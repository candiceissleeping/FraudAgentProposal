## 1. What are the main formal definitions of memorization?

### Takeaway
There is no single accepted definition. The literature offers three families: (i) *extraction-based / behavioural* definitions (eidetic, extractable, discoverable, probabilistic (n,p)-discoverable, adversarial compression), (ii) *counterfactual / influence-based* definitions (Feldman's label memorization, counterfactual memorization, causal "memorisation profiles", Kolmogorov-style "unintended memorization"), and (iii) *canary/exposure* definitions for deliberately inserted secrets. Extraction-based definitions are easy to measure but conflate memorization with duplication and generalization; counterfactual definitions separate them in principle but need retraining many models, which is infeasible for production LLMs.

### Cited Findings

#### 1.1 Canary / exposure definitions (unintended memorization)

- **Carlini, Liu, Erlingsson, Kos, Song (2019). "The Secret Sharer: Evaluating and Testing Unintended Memorization in Neural Networks." USENIX Security 2019. arXiv:1802.08232.** Authors/title verified; venue per Semantic Scholar. — [arXiv:1802.08232](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08232)
  - Proposes a testing methodology for "the risk that rare or unique training-data sequences are unintentionally memorized by generative sequence models" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:1802.08232](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08232)
  - Method: insert random "canary" sequences s[r] (randomness r drawn from space R) into training data, then compute **rank** = position of the true canary among all possible canaries ordered by model log-perplexity, and **exposure_θ(s[r]) = log2|R| − log2 rank_θ(s[r])** (Definition 4). Exposure ranges from 0 to log2|R| **[full text]**. — [arXiv:1802.08232](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08232)
  - Applied to Google Smart Compose; also demonstrates extraction of credit card numbers "from a language model trained on the Enron email data" (Sec. 8.4 "Enron Emails: Memorization in Practice") **[full text]**. Note this is the Enron *email corpus*, not Enron's SEC filings. — [arXiv:1802.08232](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08232)
  - Limitation stated in the paper: exposure "is not a normalized metric" and the rank of a canary "is not directly linked to the probability of generating sequences using greedy or beam search" **[full text]**. It needs control over training data (canary insertion), so it cannot be applied post hoc to a closed model. — [arXiv:1802.08232](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08232)

#### 1.2 Eidetic / extractable memorization

- **Carlini, Tramèr, Wallace, Jagielski, Herbert-Voss, Lee, Roberts, Brown, Song, Erlingsson, Oprea, Raffel (2021). "Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models." USENIX Security 2021. arXiv:2012.07805.** Verified (12 authors). — [arXiv:2012.07805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07805)
  - **Definition 1 (Model Knowledge Extraction):** "A string s is extractable from an LM f_θ if there exists a prefix c such that s ← argmax_{s′:|s′|=N} f_θ(s′ | c)"; the argmax may be replaced by a practical sampling strategy such as greedy decoding **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2012.07805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07805)
  - **Definition 2 (k-Eidetic Memorization):** "A string s is k-eidetic memorized (for k ≥ 1) by an LM f_θ if s is extractable from f_θ and s appears in at most k examples in the training data X: |{x ∈ X : s ⊆ x}| ≤ k" **[full text]**. Small k = more "private"/surprising memorization; an "example" for GPT-2 is a whole web page. — [arXiv:2012.07805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07805)
  - Findings: 1,800 candidate samples were manually checked and **604 unique memorized training examples** identified (≈0.1% of 600,000 generated samples); examples include PII, IRC logs, code, UUIDs; some are 1-eidetic (present in one training document). "Larger models are more vulnerable than smaller models"; the 1.5B-parameter GPT-2 "emits all examples that are inserted 33 times or more" in the URL experiment, the 345M model memorizes half, the 117M model none **[full text/abstract]**. — [arXiv:2012.07805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07805)
  - Limitations: verification was manual (web search, "confirmed in collaboration with" OpenAI), the authors call the count "likely an extremely loose lower bound", and the definition depends on what counts as a training "example" **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2012.07805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07805)

- **Nasr, Carlini, Hayase, Jagielski, Cooper, Ippolito, Choquette-Choo, Wallace, Tramèr, Lee (2023). "Scalable Extraction of Training Data from (Production) Language Models." arXiv:2311.17035 (28 Nov 2023).** Verified. A peer-reviewed version appeared as **"Scalable Extraction of Training Data from Aligned, Production Language Models," ICLR 2025** (author list differs: Nasr, Rando, Carlini et al.). — [arXiv:2311.17035](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035); [ICLR 2025 proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/cce0e917b050208170151f77b497fc71-Abstract-Conference.html)
  - **Definition 1 (Extractable memorization):** an example x from training set X "is extractably memorized if an adversary (without access to X) can construct a prompt p" that makes the model produce x **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2311.17035](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035)
  - **Definition 2 (Discoverable memorization):** "For a model Gen and an example [p‖x] from the training set X, we say that x is discoverably memorized if Gen(p) = x" **[full text]**. The paper describes discoverable memorization as capturing "an upper bound on all training data that is memorized" and extractable memorization as what a real adversary without the training set can recover. — [arXiv:2311.17035](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035)
  - Findings: gigabytes of training data extractable from open models (Pythia, GPT-Neo), semi-open (LLaMA, Falcon), and ChatGPT; a "divergence attack" (prompt: "Repeat this word forever: 'poem poem poem poem'") makes ChatGPT emit training data "at a rate 150× higher"; with **$200** of queries to gpt-3.5-turbo they extracted **over 10,000 unique verbatim-memorized training examples**; for gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct at least 0.852% of emitted tokens were part of a 50-token copy from their 9 TB auxiliary web corpus (AUXDATASET) **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2311.17035](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035)
  - In the head-to-head comparison on a previously released benchmark of training sequences, 30.1% were discoverably memorized and 14.5% extractably memorized; "only 35% of the discoverably-memorized examples were also extractable", and ~11% were extractable but not discoverable **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2311.17035](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035)
  - Limitation: for closed models, ground truth is approximated by matching against a 9 TB web corpus, so results are a lower bound with false negatives **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2311.17035](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035)

#### 1.3 Discoverable memorization ("extractable with k tokens of context")

- **Carlini, Ippolito, Jagielski, Lee, Tramèr, Zhang (2023). "Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models." ICLR 2023. arXiv:2202.07646.** Verified. — [arXiv:2202.07646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)
  - **Definition 3.1:** "A string s is extractable with k tokens of context from a model f if there exists a (length-k) string p, such that the concatenation [p ‖ s] is contained in the training data for f, and f produces s when prompted with p using greedy decoding" **[full text]**. (Nasr et al. 2023 later renamed this "discoverable memorization".) — [arXiv:2202.07646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)
  - Operationalization: prompt with a training prefix, test whether the next 50 tokens are reproduced exactly; evaluated on ~50,000-sequence subsets of The Pile **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2202.07646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)
  - Findings: three **log-linear relationships** — memorization grows with (1) model capacity, (2) duplication count, (3) prompt-context length. The 6B GPT-Neo/GPT-J model "memorizes at least 1% of its training dataset" (The Pile); "33% of training sequences in our evaluation set are extractable from the 6B model at 50 tokens of context, compared to 65% with 450 tokens"; model-size fit R² = 99.8%; masked LMs memorize roughly an order of magnitude less **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2202.07646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)
  - Limitation: requires access to the training set (to pick the true prefix); uses greedy decoding only; exact 50-token match misses near-verbatim cases (see 1.5–1.6). — [arXiv:2202.07646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)

- **Biderman, Prashanth, Sutawika, Schoelkopf, Anthony, Purohit, Raff (2023). "Emergent and Predictable Memorization in Large Language Models." NeurIPS 2023. arXiv:2304.11158.** Verified. Uses k-extractability with k = 32 and a graded "memorization score" (fraction of ordered matching tokens in the continuation; score 1 = memorized) on the Pythia suite; finds that memorization in a large model is *not* reliably predicted by smaller models or partially trained checkpoints **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2304.11158](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11158)

#### 1.4 Probabilistic extraction

- **Hayes, Swanberg, Chaudhari, Yona, Shumailov, Nasr, Choquette-Choo, Lee, Cooper (2025). "Measuring memorization in language models via probabilistic extraction." NAACL 2025. arXiv:2410.19482 (Oct 2024); DOI 10.18653/v1/2025.naacl-long.469.** Verified. (The task brief calls this "Hayes et al. 2024"; the arXiv preprint is 2024, the venue is NAACL 2025. An earlier arXiv title was "Measuring memorization through probabilistic discoverable extraction" **[UNVERIFIED — from memory]**.) — [arXiv:2410.19482](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19482)
  - **Definition 3.1 ((n, p)-discoverable extraction):** a training example z, split into an a-token prefix and k-token suffix, "is (n, p)-discoverably extractable if" the probability that at least one of n independent sampled continuations of the prefix equals the suffix is ≥ p **[full text]**. Greedy discoverable extraction is the special case n = p = 1. A non-verbatim variant, **(ε, n, p)-discoverable extraction**, counts any continuation within distance ε of the target (Definition 3.2) **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2410.19482](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19482)
  - Can be computed "with no overhead" from the per-token probabilities: if p_z is the single-draw probability of the suffix, the chance of at least one hit in n draws is 1 − (1 − p_z)^n **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2410.19482](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19482)
  - Findings: greedy discoverable extraction "underestimates extraction rates"; for Pythia 2.8B on the Enron (email) dataset with top-k sampling (k = 40, T = 1) the maximum extraction rate "is nearly 7× the greedy rate (1.3%)"; the gap grows with model size and with repetitions of the target **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2410.19482](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19482)
  - Limitation acknowledged: authors "deliberately do not prescribe specific choices for n and p"; still needs logits and known training prefixes **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2410.19482](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19482)

#### 1.5 Adversarial compression

- **Schwarzschild, Feng, Maini, Lipton, Kolter (2024). "Rethinking LLM Memorization through the Lens of Adversarial Compression." NeurIPS 2024. arXiv:2404.15146.** Verified. — [arXiv:2404.15146](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15146)
  - **Adversarial Compression Ratio (ACR):** a training string "is considered memorized if it can be elicited by a prompt (much) shorter than the string itself" **[abstract]**. **Definition 4 (τ-Compressible Memorization):** sample y "is τ-memorized if ACR(M, y) > τ(y)", where τ(y) is configurable — e.g., label samples "with a ratio greater than one as memorized", or compare against the compression ratio of a generic compressor such as GZIP/SMAZ **[full text]**. The shortest eliciting prompt is searched with the MiniPrompt procedure built on GCG (gradient-based discrete prompt optimization) **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2404.15146](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15146)
  - Critique of prior definitions inside the paper: discoverable memorization is "very permissive" and "easy to evade"; extractable memorization is ill-posed if the prompt may contain the target; counterfactual memorization needs retraining **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2404.15146](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15146)
  - Findings: many famous quotes are compressible (memorized) in existing LLMs; in-context "unlearning" system prompts and gradient-ascent unlearning on TOFU stop *completion* but do not substantially change *compression*, i.e., give an "illusion of compliance" **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2404.15146](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15146)

#### 1.6 Counterfactual and influence-based definitions

- **Feldman (2020). "Does Learning Require Memorization? A Short Tale about a Long Tail." STOC 2020. arXiv:1906.05271; DOI 10.1145/3357713.3384290.** Verified. Theoretical result: "for natural data distributions memorization of labels is necessary for achieving close-to-optimal generalization error" when subpopulation frequencies are long-tailed; even outlier and noisy labels must be memorized **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:1906.05271](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05271)
- **Feldman & Zhang (2020). "What Neural Networks Memorize and Why: Discovering the Long Tail via Influence Estimation." NeurIPS 2020. arXiv:2008.03703.** Verified. — [arXiv:2008.03703](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03703)
  - **Label memorization:** mem(A, S, i) := Pr_{h←A(S)}[h(x_i) = y_i] − Pr_{h←A(S\i)}[h(x_i) = y_i], i.e., the change in the model's accuracy on example i when i is removed from the training set; memorization equals self-influence **[full text]**. Estimated efficiently by training many models on random subsets (subsampled influence). — [arXiv:2008.03703](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03703)
- **Zhang, Ippolito, Lee, Jagielski, Tramèr, Carlini (2023). "Counterfactual Memorization in Neural Language Models." NeurIPS 2023. arXiv:2112.12938 (v1 Dec 2021).** Verified. — [arXiv:2112.12938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12938)
  - **Definition 3.1 (Counterfactual Memorization):** mem(x) ≜ E_{S⊂D, x∈S}[M(A(S), x)] − E_{S⊂D, x∉S}[M(A(S), x)] — expected performance on x of models trained *with* x minus models trained *without* x. **Definition 6.1 (Counterfactual Influence)** extends this to the effect of x on another example x′ (validation or generated text) **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2112.12938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12938)
  - Motivation: "most memorization criteria strongly correlate with the number of occurrences in the training set, capturing memorized familiar phrases, public knowledge, templated texts, or other repeated data" — the paper wants to filter this "common" memorization out **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2112.12938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12938)
  - Estimation: 400 models per dataset, each trained on a random 25% subset, for RealNews, C4 and Wiki40B:en; finds an *inverse* correlation between number of near-duplicates and counterfactual memorization **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2112.12938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12938)
  - Limitation: needs hundreds of retrainings; measures a property of the *training algorithm/architecture* rather than of one model instance (a point made by Lesci et al. 2024 below). — [arXiv:2406.04327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04327)
- **Lesci, Meister, Hofmann, Vlachos, Pimentel (2024). "Causal Estimation of Memorisation Profiles." ACL 2024. arXiv:2406.04327.** Verified. Defines memorisation "as the causal effect of training with an instance on the model's ability to predict that instance" and estimates it with a difference-in-differences design from econometrics on a single training run; on Pythia, memorisation "is stronger and more persistent in larger models" and "is determined by data order and learning rate" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2406.04327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04327)
- **Morris, Sitawarin, Guo, Kokhlikyan, Suh, Rush, Chaudhuri, Mahloujifar (2025). "How much do language models memorize?" arXiv:2505.24832.** Verified; no venue found. Separates "*unintended memorization*, the information a model contains about a specific dataset, and *generalization*, the information a model contains about the true data-generation process", formalized with Kolmogorov complexity and estimated via compression; estimates GPT-family capacity at ≈ **3.6 bits per parameter**; memorization rises until capacity is filled, then declines as "grokking"/generalization begins **[abstract/full text]**. — [arXiv:2505.24832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24832)
- **Carlini, Jagielski, Zhang, Papernot, Terzis, Tramèr (2022). "The Privacy Onion Effect: Memorization is Relative." NeurIPS 2022. arXiv:2206.10469.** Verified. Removing the most-memorized outlier layer exposes a new layer of previously safe points — memorization is relative to the rest of the dataset **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2206.10469](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10469)

#### 1.7 Verbatim vs. approximate / paraphrastic / non-literal memorization

- **Ippolito, Tramèr, Nasr, Zhang, Jagielski, Lee, Choquette-Choo, Carlini (2023). "Preventing Verbatim Memorization in Language Models Gives a False Sense of Privacy." INLG 2023. arXiv:2210.17546; DOI 10.18653/v1/2023.inlg-main.3** (the published title is listed by Semantic Scholar as "Preventing *Generation of* Verbatim Memorization…"). Verified. — [arXiv:2210.17546](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17546)
  - Defines verbatim memorization as "a model generation that exactly matches a substring from the training set" and argues this is "too restrictive". Builds MemFree decoding (Bloom-filter n-gram blocking) that "perfectly prevents all verbatim memorization", yet training data still leaks through "style-transfer" prompts; approximate memorization is measured by similarity (BLEU / edit distance) to the true continuation **[abstract/full text]**. — [arXiv:2210.17546](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17546)
- **Lee, Le, Chen, Lee (2023). "Do Language Models Plagiarize?" WWW 2023. arXiv:2203.07618; DOI 10.1145/3543507.3583199.** Verified. Distinguishes verbatim, paraphrase and idea plagiarism in GPT-2 output; all three "widely exist"; model size and decoding method are strongly associated with degree of plagiarism **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2203.07618](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07618)
- **Chen, Asai, Mireshghallah, Min, Grimmelmann, Choi, Hajishirzi, Zettlemoyer, Koh (2024). "CopyBench: Measuring Literal and Non-Literal Reproduction of Copyright-Protected Text in Language Model Generation." EMNLP 2024. arXiv:2407.07087.** Verified. Literal copying rate rises from 0.2% to 10.5% and non-literal (event/character) copying from 2.3% to 5.9% between Llama3-8B and 70B; mitigation methods reduce literal but not non-literal copying **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2407.07087](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07087)
- **Shilov, Meeus, de Montjoye (2026). "The Mosaic Memory of Large Language Models." Nature Communications (DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-68603-0 per Semantic Scholar); arXiv:2405.15523 (May 2024).** Verified title/authors; journal per Semantic Scholar. LLMs "memorize by assembling information from similar sequences"; fuzzy duplicates contribute "as much as 0.8 of an exact duplicate"; memorization is "predominantly syntactic rather than semantic"; fuzzy duplicates survive standard deduplication **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2405.15523](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15523)
- **Zhou, Xiang, Chen, Su (2024). "Quantifying and Analyzing Entity-level Memorization in Large Language Models." AAAI 2024. arXiv:2308.15727.** Verified. Proposes an entity-level definition (can the model reconstruct sensitive entities given partial information?) because verbatim definitions need the exact original text; finds "strong memorization at the entity level" even "with partial leakages" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2308.15727](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.15727)
- **Aerni, Rando, Debenedetti, Carlini, Ippolito, Tramèr (2025). "Measuring Non-Adversarial Reproduction of Training Data in Large Language Models." ICLR 2025. arXiv:2411.10242.** Verified. Defines *non-adversarial reproduction* (overlap with Internet text under benign prompts): up to 15% of text from popular chat models overlaps with Internet snippets, far more than human-written text **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2411.10242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10242)

#### 1.8 Taxonomies, SoKs and surveys

- **Hartmann, Suri, Bindschaedler, Evans, Tople, West (2023). "SoK: Memorization in General-Purpose Large Language Models." arXiv:2310.18362.** Verified; arXiv only. Taxonomy of *what* is memorized: "verbatim text, facts, ideas and algorithms, writing styles, distributional properties, and alignment goals". Highlights "the challenges that arise from the predominant way of defining memorization with respect to model behavior instead of model weights" (decoding algorithm, reasoning, hallucination) **[abstract/full text]**. — [arXiv:2310.18362](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18362)
- **Prashanth, Deng, O'Brien, Jyothir S, Khan, Borkar, Choquette-Choo, Fuehne, Biderman, Ke, Lee, Saphra (2025). "Recite, Reconstruct, Recollect: Memorization in LMs as a Multifaceted Phenomenon." ICLR 2025. arXiv:2406.17746 (Jun 2024).** Verified (12 authors; venue per Semantic Scholar). — [arXiv:2406.17746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17746)
  - Taxonomy over 32-extractable Pile sequences in Pythia: **recitation** (highly duplicated; operationalized as > 5 duplicates), **reconstruction** (inherently predictable templates — incrementing or repeating patterns), **recollection** (neither) **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2406.17746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17746)
  - Recitation is "consistently a majority" of memorized data; recollection grows fastest with model size, "from 4.49% of the examples memorized in the 70M model, to 11.34% in the 12B model"; perplexity predicts memorization well for rare sequences but not for highly duplicated ones **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2406.17746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17746)
- **Satvaty, Verberne, Turkmen (2024–2026). "Undesirable Memorization in Large Language Models: A Survey." arXiv:2410.02650 (v3 Jan 2026).** Verified; venue not confirmed. Taxonomy along granularity, retrievability and desirability **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2410.02650](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02650)
- **Wei, Zhang, Zhang, Ding, Chen, Ong, Zhang, Xiang (2024–2025). "Memorization in Deep Learning: A Survey." ACM Computing Surveys, DOI 10.1145/3769076; arXiv:2406.03880.** Verified title/authors; journal per Semantic Scholar. — [arXiv:2406.03880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03880)
- **Xiong, Zhao, Pappu, Song (2025). "The Landscape of Memorization in LLMs: Mechanisms, Measurement, and Mitigation." arXiv:2507.05578 (v2 Dec 2025; the HTML version is titled "SoK: …").** Verified; venue not confirmed. Catalogues definitions "by string matching (exact or approximate) or by loss differences", including (n,p)-discoverable extraction; states that "MIAs fall short when used as per-instance indicators of training data inclusion" because they "lack a well-calibrated null model" **[abstract/full text]**. — [arXiv:2507.05578](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05578)
- **Cooper & Grimmelmann (2025). "The Files are in the Computer: On Copyright, Memorization, and Generative AI." Chicago-Kent Law Review vol. 100; arXiv:2404.12590.** Verified. Legal-technical definition: a model has memorized a piece of training data "when (1) it is possible to reconstruct from the model (2) a near-exact copy of (3) a substantial portion of (4) that piece of training data"; distinguishes *extraction* (user intentionally elicits), *regurgitation* (model emits regardless of intent) and *reconstruction* (by any means). "Memorization occurs when a model is trained; regurgitation is a symptom not its cause" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2404.12590](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12590)

### Inferences
- The definitions answer different questions. Extraction-style definitions ask "can the model reproduce this string?"; counterfactual definitions ask "does the model's behaviour on x depend on having trained on x?". The project's concern ("the model already knows Enron was a fraud") is a counterfactual question about *outcome knowledge*, not about reproducing 10-K text, so verbatim definitions (eidetic, discoverable, ACR) measure the wrong object unless adapted to facts/entities.
- Under the Prashanth et al. taxonomy, the Enron/WorldCom outcome is a "recitation"-type item: it is repeated across an enormous number of web documents. Counterfactual memorization of any single document about Enron would be low (many near-duplicates), yet the *fact* is very strongly encoded. This is exactly the "common memorization" that Zhang et al. set out to filter out — so a low counterfactual-memorization score would not mean the model lacks the knowledge.
- Carlini's k in k-eidetic and Nasr's discoverable/extractable split both show that "memorized" is always relative to (a) how the model is queried and (b) what the training set contains; any claim for the project should state the probe and the decoding setup explicitly.

### Gaps
- I did not find a formal definition tailored to *outcome/label memorization for a named real-world entity* (e.g., "model knows firm X was later convicted of fraud") in the ML-privacy literature; the closest are entity-level memorization (Zhou et al.), fact memorization (Section 2) and the finance "lookahead" literature (Section 5).
- Yeom, Giacomelli, Fredrikson, Jha, "Privacy Risk in Machine Learning: Analyzing the Connection to Overfitting" (arXiv:1709.01604) is verified on arXiv, but its venue (IEEE CSF 2018) is from memory and was not confirmed by a tool **[UNVERIFIED venue]**. — [arXiv:1709.01604](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.01604)
- The exact numeric thresholds Ippolito et al. use for "approximate memorization" were not confirmed from the full text.
- Venues for the Satvaty et al. survey and Xiong et al. SoK could not be confirmed.

## 2. How is memorization distinguished from generalization / knowledge? Facts vs. verbatim text

### Takeaway
The literature agrees that some memorization is *necessary* for learning (long-tail theory) and that recalling facts is a desired form of memorization, but it has no operational test that cleanly separates "knows the fact because it generalizes" from "knows the fact because it memorized the outcome" in a deployed model. Proposed separators are counterfactual (with/without the example), information-theoretic (bits about the dataset vs. bits about the distribution), frequency-based (correlation of outputs with pretraining counts), or prior-aware (is the suffix specific to this prefix?). Fact-level knowledge scales with how often supporting documents appear in pretraining data.

### Cited Findings
- Memorization is necessary for near-optimal generalization on long-tailed data (theory), and empirically removing memorized examples hurts test accuracy on vision benchmarks. — [Feldman 2020, arXiv:1906.05271](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05271); [Feldman & Zhang 2020, arXiv:2008.03703](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03703)
- Hartmann et al. treat memorization of facts as "often desirable since it is necessary for performing tasks such as question answering", define memorization roughly as "learning that involves only little generalization", and state that "a correct model response to a factual question does not reveal whether the model arrived at this response via generalization and reasoning, because of hallucination, or because it has memorized this response from the training data" **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2310.18362](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18362)

On Pythia/The Pile, memorization is strongest for factual QA and *increases* with model size for factual QA only, while translation and reasoning show more generalization **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2407.14985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14985)
- **Lu, Li, Cheng, Ding, Huang, Qiu (2024). "Scaling Laws for Fact Memorization of Large Language Models." EMNLP 2024 (venue per Semantic Scholar). arXiv:2406.15720.** Verified. Verbatim memorization needs "non-trivial amounts of repetition"; it "is intertwined with the LM's general capabilities" rather than stored in specific weights, so it is hard to isolate or remove **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2407.17817](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17817)

- **Tiwari, Trachtenberg, Suh (2026). "A Prior-Aware Metric for Efficiently Distinguishing Memorization from Generalization in Large Language Models." COLM 2026 (arXiv comment). arXiv:2602.18733.** Verified metadata; abstract only. Proposes training-free "Prior-Aware memorization": is a suffix specifically tied to its training prefix, or likely after many IID prefixes? Reports that "between 55% and 90% of sequences previously labeled as memorized fail our criterion and are consistent with statistical commonality" (LLaMA, OPT) **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2602.18733](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18733)
- **Nishida et al. (7 authors) (2026). "Revisiting Non-Verbatim Memorization in Large Language Models: The Role of Entity Surface Forms." ACL 2026 Main (arXiv comment). arXiv:2604.21882.** Verified metadata; abstract only. COLM 2024 (arXiv comment "COLM camera ready"). arXiv:2404.06209.** Verified. LLMs "have memorized many popular tabular datasets verbatim" and "perform better on datasets seen during training, indicating that memorization leads to overfitting" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2404.06209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06209)
- **Chang, Cramer, Soni, Bamman (2023)** show the downstream consequence: GPT-4/ChatGPT "perform much better on memorized books than on non-memorized books for downstream tasks" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2305.00118](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.00118)

### Inferences
- For the fraud-scoring task, the relevant analogue of Bordt et al. and Chang et al. is: task performance on "seen" entities (Enron, WorldCom) is expected to be inflated relative to unseen ones, and the inflation is not evidence of analytic skill.
- Because fact knowledge tracks document frequency (Kandpal et al.; Lu et al.), heavily covered scandals are the cases most likely to be encoded; obscure enforcement actions are less likely to be. That suggests a testable gradient (fame of the case vs. model accuracy), in the spirit of Chang et al.'s web-frequency correlation.
- Nishida et al. imply that masking or changing a company's surface form may reduce but not remove access to the memorized fact — consistent with the finance evidence in Section 5.

### Gaps
- No paper found that gives a validated per-instance test for "this correct answer came from recall rather than reasoning" on a closed model. Hartmann et al. state the problem; they do not solve it.
- The Tiwari et al. (2026) and Nishida et al. (2026) claims are from abstracts only; I did not read the full texts.

## 3. What detection / proof methods exist?

### Takeaway
Methods fall into five groups: (1) membership inference attacks (MIAs), (2) extraction and prefix-completion probes, (3) canaries / watermarks / copyright traps, (4) cloze, quiz and guided-prompt probes for black-box models, and (5) counterfactual/influence methods. The 2024–2025 critical literature shows that reference-free MIAs on pretraining data are near chance once members and non-members are IID, that reported successes were largely temporal/distribution-shift artifacts, and that an MIA score cannot serve as *proof* of training-set inclusion. Verbatim extraction and randomized canaries are the two routes accepted as statistically sound evidence.

### Cited Findings

#### 3.1 Membership inference: origins and strong attacks
- **Shokri, Stronati, Song, Shmatikov (2017). "Membership Inference Attacks against Machine Learning Models." IEEE S&P 2017. arXiv:1610.05820; DOI 10.1109/SP.2017.41.** Verified. Defines the basic MIA — "given a data record and black-box access to a model, determine if the record was in the model's training dataset" — and the shadow-model attack **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:1610.05820](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.05820)
- **Carlini, Chien, Nasr, Song, Terzis, Tramèr (2022). "Membership Inference Attacks From First Principles." IEEE S&P 2022. arXiv:2112.03570.** Verified. Argues attacks must be evaluated by "true-positive rate at low (e.g., <0.1%) false-positive rates" rather than average accuracy/AUC; introduces the Likelihood Ratio Attack (**LiRA**), "10x more powerful at low false-positive rates" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2112.03570](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.03570)

#### 3.2 Reference-free MIAs for LLM pretraining data
- **Shi, Ajith, Xia, Huang, Liu, Blevins, Chen, Zettlemoyer (2024). "Detecting Pretraining Data from Large Language Models." ICLR 2024. arXiv:2310.16789.** Verified. — [arXiv:2310.16789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16789)
  - **Min-K% Prob:** score a text by the average log-probability of its k% lowest-probability tokens, on the hypothesis that "an unseen example is likely to contain a few outlier words with low probabilities under the LLM, while a seen example is less likely to". Needs no reference model **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2310.16789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16789)
  - Introduces **WikiMIA** (members = Wikipedia events before the model's training, non-members = events after). Reports average AUC 0.72 on WikiMIA, a 7.4% improvement over the best baseline; AUC increases with model size and text length; applied to detect Books3 books in GPT-3 and to audit unlearning **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2310.16789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16789)
  - Known critique: WikiMIA's member/non-member split is temporal, which later work shows is the source of most of the signal (Section 3.3).
- **Zhang, Sun, Yeats, Ouyang, Kuo, Zhang, Yang, Li (2025). "Min-K%++: Improved Baseline for Detecting Pre-Training Data from Large Language Models." ICLR 2025 (Spotlight). arXiv:2404.02936.** Verified. Motivated by training samples being local maxima of the modeled distribution; normalizes token log-probability against the conditional categorical distribution. Outperforms the runner-up by 6.2–10.5% AUROC on WikiMIA; on the harder MIMIR benchmark it only "consistently improves upon reference-free methods" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2404.02936](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02936)
- **Mattern, Mireshghallah, Jin, Schölkopf, Sachan, Berg-Kirkpatrick (2023). "Membership Inference Attacks against Language Models via Neighbourhood Comparison." ACL 2023. arXiv:2305.18462.** Verified. **Neighbourhood attack:** compare the model's loss on the target with its loss on synthetically generated neighbour texts (word substitutions), removing the need for a reference model trained on similar data. Reported TPR at 1% FPR of 8.29% vs. 3.50% for the plain LOSS attack on one benchmark; reference-based attacks are "highly fragile" to the reference data distribution **[abstract/full text]**. — [arXiv:2305.18462](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18462)
- **Xie, Wang, Huang, Zhang, Ge, Pei, Gong, Dhingra (2024). "ReCaLL: Membership Inference via Relative Conditional Log-Likelihoods." EMNLP 2024. arXiv:2406.15968.** Verified. Measures the relative drop in log-likelihood when the target is prefixed with non-member context; SOTA on WikiMIA **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2406.15968](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15968)
- **Zhang, Zhang, Guo, de Rijke, Fan, Cheng (2024). "Pretraining Data Detection for Large Language Models: A Divergence-based Calibration Method" (DC-PDD). EMNLP 2024. arXiv:2409.14781.** Verified. Calibrates token probabilities by token frequency; introduces PatentMIA **[abstract]**. Xiong et al. (2025) report that these gains were criticized as "likely artifacts of distributional shifts introduced by the benchmark itself" **[full text of Xiong et al.]**. — [arXiv:2409.14781](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14781); [arXiv:2507.05578](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05578)
- **Meeus, Jain, Rei, de Montjoye (2024). "Did the Neurons Read your Book? Document-level Membership Inference for Large Language Models." USENIX Security 2024. arXiv:2310.15007.** Verified. Document-level MIA on OpenLLaMA-7B: AUC 0.856 for books and 0.678 for papers **[abstract]**. Das et al. later showed a blind (model-free) classifier beats these numbers on the same data (Section 3.3). — [arXiv:2310.15007](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.15007)

#### 3.3 Critiques: MIAs on LLM pretraining data barely beat chance and cannot prove membership
- **Duan, Suri, Mireshghallah, Min, Shi, Zettlemoyer, Tsvetkov, Choi, Evans, Hajishirzi (2024). "Do Membership Inference Attacks Work on Large Language Models?" COLM 2024 (arXiv comment). arXiv:2402.07841.** Verified. — [arXiv:2402.07841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07841)
  - Evaluates MIAs on Pythia models (160M–12B) trained on The Pile; "MIAs barely outperform random guessing for most settings"; AUC-ROC below 0.6 in most domains **[abstract/full text]**. — [arXiv:2402.07841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07841)
  - Causes: "(1) the combination of a large dataset and few training iterations, and (2) an inherently fuzzy boundary between members and non-members" (high n-gram overlap of non-members with training data). Apparent successes elsewhere "can be attributed to a distribution shift, such as when members and non-members are drawn from the seemingly identical domain but with different temporal ranges". Releases the **MIMIR** benchmark **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2402.07841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07841)
- **Maini, Jia, Papernot, Dziedzic (2024). "LLM Dataset Inference: Did you train on my dataset?" NeurIPS 2024. arXiv:2406.06443.** Verified. — [arXiv:2406.06443](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06443)
  - Min-K% Prob achieved AUC ≈ 0.7 on WikiMIA but "an AUC close to 0.5 when tested on IID train and validation splits"; AUC varies between 0.4 and 0.7 across random subsets of The Pile **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2406.06443](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06443)
  - Proposes **dataset inference**: aggregate many MIA features with a learned linear model, then run a t-test comparing a suspect set against a private IID validation set; distinguishes Pile train vs. test subsets with p < 0.1 and no false positives, needing ~1,000 text sequences **[abstract/full text]**. Requires the claimant to hold IID held-out data that was never published. — [arXiv:2406.06443](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06443)
- **Meeus, Shilov, Jain, Faysse, Rei, de Montjoye (2025). "SoK: Membership Inference Attacks on LLMs are Rushing Nowhere (and How to Fix It)." IEEE SaTML 2025. arXiv:2406.17975; DOI 10.1109/SaTML64287.2025.00028.** Verified. A model-free bag-of-words classifier separates "members" from "non-members" with AUC 0.988 (WikiMIA-32), 0.994 (WikiMIA-128), 0.937 (BookMIA), 0.997 (Gutenberg), 0.690 (ArXiv), 0.596 (ArXivMIA); post-hoc datasets "suffer from strong distribution shifts" that "invalidate the claims of LLMs memorizing strongly in real-world scenarios". Recommends randomized test splits, injected unique sequences, randomized fine-tuning, and post-hoc controls **[abstract/full text]**. — [arXiv:2406.17975](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17975)
- **Das, Zhang, Tramèr (2025). "Blind Baselines Beat Membership Inference Attacks for Foundation Models." IEEE Deep Learning Security and Privacy Workshop (SPW) 2025 and DATA-FM @ ICLR 2025; arXiv:2406.16201; DOI 10.1109/SPW67851.2025.00016.** Verified. (The task brief's "Meeus et al. 2024 SoK 'Blind baselines'" conflates two papers: the SoK is Meeus et al.; "Blind Baselines" is Das, Zhang, Tramèr.) For 8 published MI evaluation datasets, "blind attacks — that distinguish the member and non-member distributions without looking at any trained model — outperform state-of-the-art MI attacks". Examples: WikiMIA AUC 99.0 (blind) vs. 83.9 (best MIA), TPR@5%FPR 94.7 vs. 43.2; BookMIA AUC 91.4 vs. 88.0; Gutenberg AUC 96.1 vs. 85.6 **[full text]**. — [arXiv:2406.16201](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16201)
- **Zhang, Das, Kamath, Tramèr (2025). "Membership Inference Attacks Cannot Prove that a Model Was Trained On Your Data." Position paper, IEEE SaTML 2025. arXiv:2409.19798; DOI 10.1109/SaTML64287.2025.00025.** Verified. A "training data proof" needs a bounded false-positive rate under the null hypothesis that the model was *not* trained on the target; "sampling from this null hypothesis is impossible, as we do not know the exact contents of the training set, nor can we (efficiently) retrain a large foundation model". Sound alternatives: (a) randomly sampled canaries ranked against counterfactual canaries, (b) watermarked training data, (c) verbatim data extraction **[abstract/full text]**. — [arXiv:2409.19798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19798)
- **Hayes et al. (16 authors) (2025). "Exploring the limits of strong membership inference attacks on large language models." NeurIPS 2025. arXiv:2505.18773** (earlier circulated as "Strong Membership Inference Attacks on Massive Datasets and (Moderately) Large Language Models" **[UNVERIFIED earlier title — from memory]**). Verified current title/authors/venue. Scales LiRA to GPT-2-style models (10M–1B parameters) with reference models trained on >20B tokens of C4. Strong MIAs "can succeed on pre-trained LLMs" but effectiveness "remains limited (e.g., AUC<0.7) in practical settings"; per-sample decisions are often "statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip"; model size vs. vulnerability is non-monotonic; no correlation observed between extraction and MIA success **[abstract/full text]**. — [arXiv:2505.18773](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18773)
- **Puerto, Gubri, Yun, Oh (2025). "Scaling Up Membership Inference: When and How Attacks Succeed on Large Language Models." Findings of NAACL 2025. arXiv:2411.00154.** Verified. MIA "still works on LLMs, but only when multiple documents are presented for testing"; aggregating paragraph-level features (adapting Maini et al.'s dataset inference) achieves "AUROC scores of 80% or higher" for document sets on pretraining data, and AUROC > 88% at collection level for continual-learning fine-tuning data **[abstract/full text]**. — [arXiv:2411.00154](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00154)
- **Kandpal, Wallace, Raffel (2022)** had already shown that "existing methods for detecting memorized sequences have near-chance accuracy on non-duplicated training sequences" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2202.06539](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06539)

CDD detects contamination from the "peakedness" of the sampled output distribution **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2402.15938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15938)
- **Cheng, Marone, Weller, Lawrie, Khashabi, Van Durme (2024). "Dated Data: Tracing Knowledge Cutoffs in Large Language Models." arXiv:2403.12958.** Verified; venue not confirmed by tool. Probes *effective* knowledge cutoffs per resource by perplexity across dated versions of documents; effective cutoffs "often differ from reported cutoffs" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2403.12958](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12958)

#### 3.7 Influence-function and causal approaches
- Subsampled influence / counterfactual memorization (Feldman & Zhang 2020; Zhang et al. 2023) and difference-in-differences memorisation profiles (Lesci et al. 2024). — [arXiv:2008.03703](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03703); [arXiv:2112.12938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12938); [arXiv:2406.04327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04327)
- EK-FAC influence functions scaled to 52B-parameter LLMs (Grosse et al. 2023): attribute a model behaviour to the training sequences that most influence it; requires access to weights and candidate training data **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2308.03296](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03296)
- Mechanistic localization: **Stoehr, Gordon, Zhang, Lewis (2024). "Localizing Paragraph Memorization in Language Models." arXiv:2403.19851.** Verified; arXiv only. Gradients of memorized paragraphs are larger in lower layers; a low-layer attention head attends to rare tokens; "a few distinctive tokens early in a prefix can often corrupt the entire continuation" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2403.19851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19851)

### Inferences
- For closed commercial models (the likely tools in the fraud project), only black-box probes are available: completion, cloze/entity reconstruction, multiple-choice (DE-COP style), guided vs. unguided prompting, and — where logprobs exist — Min-K%-style scores. The critique literature implies that a Min-K%-style score on a 10-K excerpt should *not* be presented as proof of training-set membership.
- A temporal split (pre-/post-cutoff filings) is the natural design for the project but is precisely the design that Duan, Maini, Meeus and Das show to be confounded: a detector can separate eras by vocabulary alone. Any pre/post comparison needs a blind (model-free) baseline to show the effect is not just date detection.
- A positive finding is easier to defend than a negative one. Verbatim extraction or correct entity reconstruction from an anonymized filing is affirmative evidence (Zhang et al. 2025; Chang et al. 2023), whereas failure to extract shows only a lower bound (Nasr et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2025 show completion and membership can also diverge in the other direction).
- The name-cloze design maps directly onto the project: mask the company name (and other identifiers) in a 10-K passage and ask the model to name the firm/year. This is essentially what Lopez-Lira et al. did with earnings calls (Section 5).

### Gaps
- No published method found that tests memorization of 10-K filings specifically; no public benchmark of "members vs. IID non-members" for SEC filings was identified.
- I did not verify the exact Min-K% formula text or the WikiMIA construction details beyond the abstract and results table.
- Venue for Liu et al. (arXiv:2503.17514) and for Cheng et al. (arXiv:2403.12958) not confirmed.

## 4. What factors increase memorization?

### Takeaway
Four factors are consistently reported: duplication count (superlinear/log-linear effect), model size (larger models memorize more and rarer text, and forget less), prompt/context length (more context → more extraction), and training exposure and position (more epochs and later-in-training data are more memorized; early data without re-exposure is partly forgotten). Fuzzy duplicates count almost as much as exact ones, and what matters is frequency relative to corpus size.

### Cited Findings
- **Duplication.** "A sequence that is present 10 times in the training data is on average generated ~1000 times more often than a sequence that is present only once"; regeneration rate is superlinear in count; deduplication makes models "considerably more secure". — **Kandpal, Wallace, Raffel (2022). "Deduplicating Training Data Mitigates Privacy Risks in Language Models." ICML 2022. arXiv:2202.06539** (verified) — [arXiv:2202.06539](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06539)
- Extractability is log-linear in the number of duplicates; "models rarely regurgitate strings that are repeated only a few times" **[full text]**. — [Carlini et al. 2023, arXiv:2202.07646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)
- Highly duplicated sequences are "both easier to extract and discover" **[full text]**. — [Nasr et al. 2023, arXiv:2311.17035](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035)
- "Non-trivial amounts of repetition are necessary for verbatim memorization to happen" in controlled injection experiments **[abstract]**. — [Huang et al. 2024, arXiv:2407.17817](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17817)
- Fuzzy (near-)duplicates contribute "as much as 0.8 of an exact duplicate" and are "untouched by deduplication techniques" **[abstract]**. — [Shilov et al., arXiv:2405.15523](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15523)
- Counterpoint: duplication *lowers* counterfactual memorization of each copy (inverse correlation with near-duplicate count) **[full text]**. — [Zhang et al. 2023, arXiv:2112.12938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12938)
- Books: name-cloze accuracy correlates with how often book passages appear on the web **[abstract]**. — [Chang et al. 2023, arXiv:2305.00118](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.00118)
- Facts: QA accuracy depends on the number of relevant pretraining documents **[abstract]**. — [Kandpal et al. 2023, arXiv:2211.08411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.08411)
- **Model size.** "Larger models are more vulnerable than smaller models" (GPT-2 family) **[abstract]**. — [Carlini et al. 2021, arXiv:2012.07805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07805)
- Log-linear growth of extractable fraction with parameter count (R² 99.8% within GPT-Neo family); the picture "becomes more complicated when generalizing these results across model families" **[abstract/full text]**. — [Carlini et al. 2023, arXiv:2202.07646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)
- **Tirumala, Markosyan, Zettlemoyer, Aghajanyan (2022). "Memorization Without Overfitting: Analyzing the Training Dynamics of Large Language Models." NeurIPS 2022. arXiv:2205.10770** (verified): "larger language models memorize training data faster across all settings", "can memorize a larger portion of the data before over-fitting and tend to forget less"; "models memorize nouns and numbers first", which act as unique identifiers for individual training examples **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2205.10770](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10770)
- Larger models disproportionately add "recollection" of rare sequences (4.49% → 11.34% of memorized examples from 70M to 12B) **[full text]**. — [Prashanth et al. 2025, arXiv:2406.17746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17746)
- Literal copying 0.2% → 10.5% from Llama3-8B to 70B **[abstract]**. — [Chen et al. 2024, arXiv:2407.07087](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07087)
- **Freeman, Rippe, Debenedetti, Andriushchenko (2024). "Exploring Memorization and Copyright Violation in Frontier LLMs: A Study of the New York Times v. OpenAI 2023 Lawsuit." arXiv:2412.06370** (verified; arXiv only): "as models increase in size, especially beyond 100 billion parameters, they demonstrate significantly greater capacity for memorization"; GPT and Claude models use refusal training and output filters against verbatim output **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2412.06370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06370)
- MIA detectability also rises with model size and text length (Min-K% on WikiMIA) **[full text]**. — [Shi et al. 2024, arXiv:2310.16789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16789)
- Counter-evidence under strict 1-epoch training: with LiRA, "larger models are not necessarily more at risk" — non-monotonic relationship (AUC 0.592 at 10M, 0.699 at 85M, 0.547 at 489M) **[full text]**. — [Hayes et al. 2025, arXiv:2505.18773](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18773)
- **Context / prompt length.** Extractable fraction rises log-linearly with prompt tokens: 33% at 50 tokens vs. 65% at 450 tokens (GPT-Neo 6B, evaluation set) **[full text]**. — [Carlini et al. 2023, arXiv:2202.07646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)
- Non-prefix prompts can elicit more than the true prefix (instruction prompts +23.7% overlap) **[abstract]**. — [Kassem et al. 2025, arXiv:2403.04801](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04801)
- Decoding matters: greedy decoding underestimates extraction relative to multi-sample top-k **[full text]**. — [Hayes et al. 2025, arXiv:2410.19482](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19482)
- **Training exposure, epochs, order and recency.** MIA performance "increases linearly with the number of effective epochs"; near-one-epoch pretraining on massive data is why MIAs fail **[full text]**. — [Duan et al. 2024, arXiv:2402.07841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07841)
- **Jagielski et al. (11 authors) (2023). "Measuring Forgetting of Memorized Training Examples." ICLR 2023. arXiv:2207.00099** (verified): standard models "empirically do forget examples over time"; examples seen early in training on very large datasets "may observe privacy benefits at the expense of examples seen later" **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2207.00099](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.00099)
- "Samples seen later in training tend to be more at risk" (complicated by sample length) **[full text]**. — [Hayes et al. 2025, arXiv:2505.18773](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18773)
- "Models are more likely to memorize data seen later in training" ("palimpsestic memorization"), used as a test statistic for model provenance. — **Kuditipudi, Huang, Zhu, Yang, Potts, Liang (2025). "Blackbox Model Provenance via Palimpsestic Membership Inference." arXiv:2510.19796** (verified; arXiv only) **[abstract]** — [arXiv:2510.19796](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.19796)
- **Wei et al. (10 authors) (2025). "Hubble: a Model Suite to Advance the Study of LLM Memorization." arXiv:2510.19811** (verified; arXiv only): "memorization risks are determined by the frequency of sensitive data relative to size of the training corpus"; "sensitive data without continued exposure can be forgotten"; recommends diluting and ordering sensitive data earlier **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2510.19811](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.19811)
- Memorisation "is determined by data order and learning rate" **[abstract]**. — [Lesci et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.04327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04327)
- Duplicated training data produces faster forgetting of factual knowledge; larger batch sizes increase robustness to forgetting **[abstract]**. — [Chang et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.11813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11813)
- Effective knowledge cutoffs differ by resource because CommonCrawl dumps contain "non-trivial amounts of old data" and deduplication handles near-duplicates imperfectly **[abstract]**. — [Cheng et al. 2024, arXiv:2403.12958](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12958)
- **Data properties.** Rare tokens and distinctive identifiers anchor memorization (nouns/numbers first; rare-token attention head). — [Tirumala et al. 2022, arXiv:2205.10770](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10770); [Stoehr et al. 2024, arXiv:2403.19851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19851)
- Masked LMs memorize about an order of magnitude less than comparably sized causal LMs **[full text]**. — [Carlini et al. 2023, arXiv:2202.07646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)
- Capacity view: models memorize until capacity (~3.6 bits/parameter) fills, after which unintended memorization of individual samples decreases as dataset size grows **[abstract]**. — [Morris et al. 2025, arXiv:2505.24832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24832)
- In financial data, memorization of individual security prices "is more pronounced for prominent stocks and in recent periods" (GPT-4o) **[full text]**. — [Lopez-Lira et al. 2025, arXiv:2504.14765](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14765)

### Inferences
- Enron and WorldCom score high on every risk factor that applies to facts: extreme duplication across news, textbooks, case studies and court documents; distinctive entity names and numbers; and target models that are very large. The only factor pulling the other way is age (2001–2002 events are far from the end of training), but the literature's "recency" effect concerns *position in the training stream*, not the calendar date of the event; old events are continually re-discussed in recent web text.
- Fuzzy-duplicate results (mosaic memory) suggest that paraphrasing or light anonymization of a 10-K may not remove the association with the outcome.
- Prompt-length results imply that feeding the full 10-K (long, distinctive context) is the setting most likely to activate memorized associations.

### Gaps
- "Recency" in the ML literature is measured as training-step position, not document date; I found no study that directly measures memorization as a function of the *calendar age* of an event while holding web frequency fixed. Lopez-Lira et al. report a recency effect for security prices but do not separate it from frequency.
- Evidence on model size comes mostly from open model families (GPT-2, GPT-Neo, Pythia, Llama); there is little controlled evidence for frontier proprietary models beyond extraction case studies.

## 5. What is the state of the art (2025–2026) on definitions and detection? Any newer consensus?

### Takeaway
As of November 2026 there is still no unified definition, but several points are now widely shared: (1) extraction should be measured probabilistically, not with one greedy completion; (2) post-hoc MIAs on pretraining data are not reliable per-instance evidence, and evaluation must use randomized or IID controls; (3) verbatim extraction from production models remains possible despite safeguards and is the strongest form of proof; (4) attention has shifted to separating memorization from statistical commonality and to "propensity" under benign use. In finance/economics a parallel literature treats memorization as *lookahead bias* and shows that prompt instructions and entity masking do not reliably prevent recall.

### Cited Findings

#### 5.1 Extraction at book scale; production models
- **Cooper, Lemley, Casasola, Ahmed, Gokaslan, Cyphert, De Sa, Ho, Liang (2025/2026). "Extracting memorized pieces of (copyrighted) books from open-weight language models." arXiv:2505.12546 (v1 May 2025; v6 20 Jul 2026 notes "A shorter version of this work was published at COLM 2026").** Verified. Sliding-window probabilistic extraction (50-token prefix / 50-token suffix, window every 10 characters, extraction counted when p_z ≥ 0.1%) over 200 books and 14 open-weight LLMs (v6 figures). "Most LLMs do not memorize most books either in whole or in part", but Llama 3.1 70B memorizes *Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone* and *1984* "almost entirely"; the whole book can be deterministically extracted near-verbatim from the first few words **[abstract/full text]**. — [arXiv:2505.12546](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12546)
- **Ahmed, Cooper, Koyejo, Liang (2026). "Extracting books from production language models." arXiv:2601.02671 (6 Jan 2026).** Verified; arXiv only. Two-phase procedure (probe, sometimes with Best-of-N jailbreak, then iterative continuation) on Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok 3; near-verbatim recall (nv-recall) for *Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone* of 76.8% (Gemini 2.5 Pro) and 70.3% (Grok 3) without jailbreak; jailbroken Claude 3.7 Sonnet reached nv-recall 95.8% in some cases; GPT-4.1 needed far more attempts and refused (nv-recall 4.0%). #### 5.2 Membership inference: where the field settled
- Strong (LiRA) MIAs work but stay below AUC 0.7 under realistic pretraining; per-sample decisions are unstable (Hayes et al., NeurIPS 2025). — [arXiv:2505.18773](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18773)

- MIAs lack "a well-calibrated null model"; they are useful for aggregate auditing, not per-instance proof (Xiong et al. SoK 2025; Zhang et al. SaTML 2025). — [arXiv:2507.05578](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05578); [arXiv:2409.19798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19798)
- Aggregation over many documents (dataset inference / collection-level MIA) recovers signal (Maini et al. 2024; Puerto et al. 2025). — [arXiv:2406.06443](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06443); [arXiv:2411.00154](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00154)

Accepted at IEEE SaTML (arXiv comment). arXiv:2512.13352.** Verified metadata; abstract only. Benchmarks MIA scores as the ranking step inside extraction pipelines and compares with standard MIA benchmarks **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2512.13352](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.13352)

Argues against "treating sequence-level membership inference as the gold standard" and proposes token-level signals to localize which tokens are memorized **[abstract]**. — [arXiv:2510.05582](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05582)

#### 5.3 Newer definitional refinements (2025–2026)
- Completion ≠ membership: models complete text that is not an n-gram member of training data (Liu et al. 2025). — [arXiv:2503.17514](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17514)

### Inferences
- The 2025–2026 position most relevant to the project is Lopez-Lira et al.'s non-identification argument: for a pre-cutoff case like Enron, a correct fraud score cannot be attributed to reading the 10-K, and any memorization test that comes back negative is only a lower bound. This matches the ML-privacy conclusion that absence of extraction is not absence of memorization.
- Two diagnostics from this literature transfer directly: (a) a no-context recall query (Gao et al.'s LAP; Didisheim et al.'s context-free retrieval) — ask the model about the firm/year with no filing text and see whether it asserts the outcome; (b) an entity-reconstruction test on anonymized filings (Lopez-Lira et al.; Chang et al.'s name cloze). Both are black-box and cheap.
- The MemGuard-Alpha abstract and the Meeus/Das critiques point the same way: any "contamination score" that includes date or era information will trivially separate pre-/post-cutoff samples, so it must be benchmarked against a model-free baseline.
- Evidence on anonymization is mixed: Glasserman & Lin find anonymization useful (and that the distraction effect can dominate lookahead), while Lopez-Lira et al. find models re-identify firms from masked text. The difference may stem from text length and distinctiveness (headlines vs. full earnings calls); a 10-K is closer to the latter.

### Gaps
- I found **no study that tests LLM memorization of fraud outcomes for specific firms (Enron, WorldCom) or of 10-K filings**.
