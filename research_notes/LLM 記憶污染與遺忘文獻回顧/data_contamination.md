## 1. How is contamination defined and categorized? (primary papers + survey taxonomies)

### Takeaway
There is no single definition: the literature has moved from "the test split appeared verbatim in pre-training data" (Magar & Schwartz; Sainz et al.) toward broader notions — task contamination (Li & Flanigan), indirect leakage through API usage (Balloccu et al.), semantic/information-level exposure (Xu et al.), and finally a purely *outcome-based* definition, "artificially inflated, non-generalizing performance" (Dekoninck et al., ConStat). For the 10-K fraud project the relevant categories are not classic test-set leakage but *raw-text contamination plus label/outcome exposure* (Sainz), *semantic/information-level* BDC (Xu), and what finance authors call *functional look-ahead bias* (Section 7).

### Cited Findings

**Foundational / position papers**

- **Magar & Schwartz (2022), "Data Contamination: From Memorization to Exploitation"**, ACL 2022 (per arXiv comment "Accepted to ACL 2022"), arXiv:2203.08242 **[V-arXiv]**. Separates two effects: *memorization* (`mem`: difference in MLM accuracy at predicting the label of seen vs. unseen contaminated instances, measured after pre-training) and *exploitation* (`expl`: difference in downstream task performance on seen vs. unseen instances after fine-tuning). Setup: BERT pre-trained on Wikipedia + labelled downstream data (SST-5, SST-2, SNLI). — [arXiv 2203.08242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08242)
  - Both `mem` and `expl` grow with the number of duplicates, "reaching 60% mem and almost 40% expl when it appears 200 times"; low `mem` (≤10%) gives no `expl`, but high `mem` does not guarantee `expl` (BERT-base + 600M Wikipedia tokens + SST-5: 15% `mem`, <1% `expl`). Conclusion: memorization is not a sufficient condition for exploitation. — [arXiv 2203.08242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08242)
  - Limitation: BERT-scale MLM models and classification tasks; authors themselves ask whether results generalize to larger models. — [arXiv 2203.08242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08242)
- **Sainz, Campos, García-Ferrero, Etxaniz, Lopez de Lacalle, Agirre (2023), "NLP Evaluation in trouble: On the Need to Measure LLM Data Contamination for each Benchmark"**, Findings of EMNLP 2023 (Singapore, Dec 2023), pp. 10776–10787, arXiv:2310.18018 **[V]**. *Citation flag:* the arXiv "Comments" field reads "Accepted at EMNLP2024-Findings", but the ACL Anthology entry is `2023.findings-emnlp.722` — cite as Findings of EMNLP 2023. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.722/); [arXiv 2310.18018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18018)
  - General definition: "data contamination refers to any breach in the strict control of datasets required by the experimental protocol." Three types: **guideline contamination** (annotation guidelines seen by the model — matters for zero/few-shot claims), **raw text contamination** (the un-annotated source text, e.g. Wikipedia, was seen), **annotation contamination** (labels exposed; if the evaluation split is involved "the experiment is completely invalidated"; if train/dev splits, zero/few-shot claims are invalidated). — [arXiv 2310.18018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18018)
  - Three pipeline stages where contamination enters: pre-training, supervised/instruction fine-tuning (incl. distillation from contaminated closed teachers), and **after deployment** (providers retraining on user inputs). — [arXiv 2310.18018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18018)
  - Two consequences: over-estimated model performance, and (their main concern) wrong scientific conclusions in papers that use contaminated LLMs. Proposes a public registry of contamination evidence (LM Contamination Index) and flagging of affected papers. Limitation: position paper; the proposed memorization-based auditing is "totally manual". — [arXiv 2310.18018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18018)
- **Jacovi, Caciularu, Goldman, Goldberg (2023), "Stop Uploading Test Data in Plain Text: Practical Strategies for Mitigating Data Contamination by Evaluation Benchmarks"**, EMNLP 2023, arXiv:2305.10160 **[V-arXiv]**. Frames two contamination scenarios (internet-crawled corpora; closed API models that may train on submitted inputs) and three strategies: (1) encrypt released test data with a public key and use a "No Derivatives" licence; (2) demand training-exclusion controls from closed-API providers and do not evaluate without them; (3) avoid internet data that appears with its solution, and release web-page context. — [arXiv 2305.10160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10160)
  - Stated limitation: negligent actors can re-upload plain text and defeat the strategies; "Strategy 2 is vulnerable"; licences encourage rather than enforce. — [arXiv 2305.10160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10160)
- **Li & Flanigan (2024), "Task Contamination: Language Models May Not Be Few-Shot Anymore"**, AAAI 2024, arXiv:2312.16337 **[V-arXiv]**. Distinguishes *test data contamination* ("the inclusion of test data examples and labels in the pre-training data") from **task contamination** ("the inclusion of task training examples in the pre-training data, effectively making the evaluation no longer zero or few-shot"). — [arXiv 2312.16337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.16337)
  - 12 models × 16 classification datasets (+1 semantic parsing) = 192 model/dataset pairs, 136 pre-collection and 56 post-collection; models are significantly more likely to beat the majority baseline on datasets released *before* their training-data collection date (Mann-Whitney U, 99% confidence, zero- and few-shot). For tasks with no possibility of task contamination, models "rarely demonstrate statistically significant improvements over simple majority baselines". — [arXiv 2312.16337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.16337)
  - Uses four detectors (training-data inspection, task-example extraction, membership inference, chronological analysis); authors note the first three are high-precision/low-recall ("absence of evidence … is not evidence of absence") and chronological analysis is high-recall/low-precision. Spider membership-inference: exact-match count vs. accuracy R² = 0.88. Caveat they address: newer datasets could simply be harder (they report R² = 0.001 between release date and fine-tuned-PLM performance). — [arXiv 2312.16337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.16337)
- **Balloccu, Schmidtová, Lango, Dušek (2024), "Leak, Cheat, Repeat: Data Contamination and Evaluation Malpractices in Closed-Source LLMs"**, EACL 2024 (main), arXiv:2402.03927 **[V-arXiv]**. Introduces **indirect data leaking**: benchmark data sent by researchers through interfaces whose data-usage policy permits training. Reviewed 255 papers (212 relevant); ~42% (90 papers) accessed ChatGPT via the web interface, leaking ~4.7M samples from 263 benchmarks to GPT-3.5/GPT-4 in the first year; also documents missing/unfair baselines and reproducibility problems. Limitation: quantifies *exposure opportunity* under OpenAI's policy, not proven training use. — [arXiv 2402.03927](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03927)
- **Aiyappa, An, Kwak, Ahn (2023), "Can we trust the evaluation on ChatGPT?"**, TrustNLP 2023 workshop (pp. 47–54, DOI 10.18653/v1/2023.trustnlp-1.5), arXiv:2303.12767 **[V]**. Case study (stance detection) on contamination in a closed, continuously updated model; no quantitative claim in the abstract. — [arXiv 2303.12767](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12767)
- **Palavalli, Bertsch, Gormley (2024), "A Taxonomy for Data Contamination in Large Language Models"**, CONDA workshop @ ACL 2024, arXiv:2407.08716 **[V-arXiv]**. Taxonomy of contamination types an LLM can meet in pre-training (incl. altered versions of test sets that decontamination misses); analyses impact on summarization and QA. — [arXiv 2407.08716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08716)
- **Dominguez-Olmedo, Dorner, Hardt (2024/2025), "Training on the Test Task Confounds Evaluation and Emergence"**, ICLR 2025 (Oral), arXiv:2407.07890 **[V-arXiv]**. Defines *training on the test task* — using knowledge about evaluation tasks at training time — as distinct from (and not a malpractice like) test-data leakage. Across 56 base models, models trained after Nov 2023 outperform older ones at equal compute by on average 7 points (MMLU) and 19 points (GSM8K); after fine-tuning all models on the same task data, the gap vanishes. — [arXiv 2407.07890](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07890)
- **Dekoninck, Müller, Vechev (2024), ConStat** (full entry in §2): redefines contamination by effect, "artificially inflated, non-generalizing performance", with three grades — *syntax-specific* (fails on rephrasings), *sample-specific* (fails on new samples from the same distribution), *benchmark-specific* (fails on another benchmark for the same task). — [arXiv 2405.16281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16281)
- **Sainz et al. (28 authors, 2024), "Data Contamination Report from the 2024 CONDA Shared Task"**, arXiv:2407.21530 **[V-arXiv]** (search results indicate it appeared in the CONDA 2024 workshop proceedings, pp. 41–56 — proceedings page not opened). Community database: 566 reported entries over 91 contaminated sources from 23 contributors. — [arXiv 2407.21530](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21530)

**Survey papers and their taxonomies**

- **Ravaut, Ding, Jiao, Chen, Li, Zhao, Qin, Xiong, Joty (2024→2025)**, arXiv:2404.00699 **[V-arXiv]**. *Citation flag:* v1 (31 Mar 2024) title was "How Much are LLMs Contaminated? A Comprehensive Survey and the LLMSanitize Library"; the current v5 (9 Jul 2025) title is **"A Comprehensive Survey of Contamination Detection Methods in Large Language Models"**, "Accepted by TMLR in July 2025". — [arXiv 2404.00699](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00699)
  - Formal definition: a dataset is contaminated if some evaluation item x and training item x′ have similarity f(x, x′) ≥ τ (τ = 1 is verbatim); distinguishes **input contamination** vs **input+label contamination**; access levels white-/gray-/black-box. — [arXiv 2404.00699v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.00699v5)
  - Taxonomy: **open-data detection** (string matching, e.g. GPT-2 8-grams, GPT-3 13-grams, Llama-2 10-token spans; embedding similarity; LLM paraphrase detection) vs **closed-data detection** (performance analysis incl. time-cutoff studies; membership-inference attacks; model memorization via prompting/cloze/quiz; model confidence/likelihood such as Min-K% Prob, CDD, shuffled-order test). States it reviews "more than 50 detection techniques and more than 120 relevant papers". — [arXiv 2404.00699v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.00699v5)
  - The companion **LLMSanitize** library (github `ntunlp/LLMSanitize`) implements string-matching methods (gpt-2, gpt-3, exact, palm, gpt-4 styles), embedding similarity (platypus), and closed-data methods (guided-prompting, sharded-likelihood, min-prob, cdd, ts-guessing variants) per its README as summarized by search. — [GitHub ntunlp/LLMSanitize](https://github.com/ntunlp/LLMSanitize); [arXiv 2404.00699v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.00699v1)
- **Xu, Guan, Greene, Kechadi (2024), "Benchmark Data Contamination of Large Language Models: A Survey"**, arXiv:2406.04244 **[V-arXiv; no venue found]**. Definition of BDC: "Exposure of a large language model to benchmark data during the training process leads to distorted evaluation results." Four levels of increasing severity: **semantic level** (same/derivative content or same source/topic), **information level** (benchmark metadata, time/label distributions, external reviews), **data level** (test inputs without labels), **label level** (inputs + labels). Detection = matching-based (dataset inspection, membership inference, example generation) vs comparison-based (content comparison, sequential analysis [Oren], chronological analysis [Huang; Roberts]). Mitigation = curating new data (private or dynamic benchmarks), refactoring existing data (regeneration, content filtering), benchmark-free evaluation (LLM-as-judge, human arenas). Notes that fully removing BDC is impractical and that AI-generated content pushes contamination toward the hard-to-detect semantic level. — [arXiv 2406.04244](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04244)
- **Deng, Zhao, Heng, Li, Cao, Tang, Cohan (2024), "Unveiling the Spectrum of Data Contamination in Language Models: A Survey from Detection to Remediation"**, arXiv:2406.14644, arXiv comment "ACL 2024 Camera-Ready Version" **[V-arXiv; UNVERIFIED-track: main vs Findings]**. Detection taxonomy: retrieval, temporal cutoff, masking-based (name cloze, TS-Guessing), perturbation-based (rephrasing, ConStat), canonical order (Oren), behaviour manipulation (guided prompting, quiz, CDD), membership inference. Mitigation: benchmarks from recent data, dynamic refresh (DyVal, Clean-Eval), encryption, label protection. Stance: sceptical — no ground truth for comparing black-box detectors, detectors rest on violable assumptions, detection research outpaces mitigation. — [arXiv 2406.14644](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14644)
- **Chen, Chen, Li, Jiang, Wan, He, Ran, Gu, Li, Xie, Ray (2025), "Recent Advances in Large Langauge [sic] Model Benchmarks against Data Contamination: From Static to Dynamic Evaluation"**, arXiv:2502.17521 (v2 29 Sep 2025) **[V-arXiv]**. *Citation flags:* (a) the assignment's "Cheng et al. 2025 survey on dynamic evaluation" is this paper — first author is **Simin Chen**, not Cheng; (b) search results show the published version at EMNLP 2025 (`2025.emnlp-main.511`, pp. 10080–10098) carries a different title, "Benchmarking Large Language Models Under Data Contamination: A Survey from Static to Dynamic Evaluation" (Anthology page not opened directly). — [arXiv 2502.17521](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17521)
  - Distinguishes **exact** vs **syntactic** contamination (match after paraphrase/whitespace/synonym transformation). Dynamic-benchmark families: temporal cutoff (LiveBench, LiveCodeBench, AntiLeak-Bench, ForecastBench), rule-based generation (GSM-Symbolic, DyVal, MMLU-CF), LLM-based generation (rewriting, interactive e.g. KIEval, multi-agent e.g. Benchmark Self-Evolving), hybrid (LatestEval). Proposes six criteria for dynamic benchmarks: correctness, scalability, collision, stability of complexity, diversity, interpretability — none of the surveyed benchmarks satisfies all six. — [arXiv 2502.17521v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17521v2)
- **Cheng, Chang, Wu (2025), "A Survey on Data Contamination for Large Language Models"**, arXiv:2502.14425 **[V-arXiv; no venue found]**. Contamination-free evaluation = data-updating / data-rewriting / prevention-based; detection = white-box / gray-box / black-box by model-information dependency. — [arXiv 2502.14425](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14425)
- **Fu, Uzuner, Yetisgen, Xia (2024/2025), "Does Data Contamination Detection Work (Well) for LLMs? A Survey and Evaluation on Detection Assumptions"**, Findings of NAACL 2025 (per arXiv comment), arXiv:2410.18966 **[V-arXiv]**. Defines instance-level vs dataset-level (full / partial / clean), direct vs indirect (derivatives such as paraphrases, summaries, distilled outputs), and task contamination; catalogues **eight assumption categories** behind detectors (see §2 and §6). — [arXiv 2410.18966](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18966)

### Inferences
- A 10-K → "later fraud revealed?" evaluation on pre-cutoff firms is contaminated in a way the classic taxonomies only partly name: the *input* (the filing) is public raw text (Sainz: raw-text contamination; Xu: data level), while the *label* is not stored next to the input in any benchmark file but is world knowledge spread across news, enforcement releases, textbooks (Xu: semantic/information level; Ravaut: input+label contamination with similarity τ < 1; Fu: indirect contamination). This is the hardest-to-detect corner of every taxonomy.
- Magar & Schwartz's memorization ≠ exploitation result cuts both ways for the project: showing the model "knows Enron" does not by itself prove the fraud score used that knowledge, and failing to elicit recall does not prove the score is clean.
- ConStat's outcome-based definition is the most operational for the project: "contaminated" = fraud-detection performance that does not carry over to anonymized/perturbed/post-cutoff filings.

### Gaps
- None of the surveys treats "outcome leakage" (model knows what happened *after* the input document) as a named category; the closest formal treatment is in the finance look-ahead-bias literature (§7).
- Track (main vs Findings) for Deng et al. survey could not be confirmed (Anthology not opened for it).
- LLMSanitize README was not opened directly (GitHub summary came via search); the v5 survey text read (first ~100k chars) does not mention the library, consistent with the title change, but this was a partial read.

## 2. Detection methods: what does each actually prove, and under which assumptions?

### Takeaway
Detectors fall into (i) *memorization-elicitation* tests (guided completion, quiz, masked-option guessing), (ii) *likelihood/statistical* tests (exchangeability test, Min-K%, perplexity/n-gram, output-distribution peakedness), (iii) *performance-based* tests (chronological/time-split regressions, ConStat). Type (i)/(ii) can prove *exposure/memorization* (often only verbatim, often only with log-prob access, and only at ≥ several duplicates) but not that scores were inflated; type (iii) measures *inflation* but cannot attribute it to a specific leak. A positive is informative; a negative almost never is.

### Cited Findings

**Summary table (assumptions and what is proven)**

| Method | Access needed | Core assumption | What a positive result proves | Main failure modes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Guided-instruction completion (Golchin & Surdeanu, Time Travel) | Black-box text | Model can reproduce instance text when cued with dataset name + split + prefix | Verbatim/near-verbatim memorization of dataset instances at partition level | Paraphrased or label-only leakage; does not distinguish contamination types — [arXiv 2308.08493](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08493) |
| Data Contamination Quiz (DCQ) | Black-box text | A model that saw an instance verbatim can pick it out among word-level perturbations | Verbatim exposure; gives a range estimate of contamination level | Positional bias (handled by design); only verbatim contamination — [arXiv 2311.06233](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06233) |
| TS-Guessing (Deng et al.) | Black-box text | Guessing a masked *wrong* option / unlikely word is infeasible without having seen the item | Familiarity with benchmark items | Guessable distractors; no formal error control — [arXiv 2311.09783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09783) |
| Exchangeability / sharded rank test (Oren et al.) | Log-probabilities | Dataset is exchangeable; model saw it in *canonical order* | Statistically valid (false-positive-controlled) proof the ordered dataset was in training | Shuffled or reformatted leaks; duplication count 1; API models without logprobs — [arXiv 2310.17623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17623) |
| Min-K% Prob (Shi et al.) | Token probabilities | Unseen text contains a few low-probability outlier tokens | Instance-level membership score | Near-random on real pre-training data; confounded by temporal/distribution shift — [arXiv 2310.16789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16789); [arXiv 2402.07841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07841) |
| Perplexity / N-gram accuracy (Li 2023; Xu et al. BenBench) | Log-probs or generation | Trained-on text has atypically low perplexity / high continuation accuracy vs a reference | Relative evidence of training on train vs test split | Needs a comparable reference; cannot separate train-split from test-split use without paraphrase baseline — [arXiv 2309.10677](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10677); [arXiv 2404.18824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18824) |
| CDD (Dong et al.) | Black-box sampling | Contaminated prompts give a *peaked* output distribution | Memorized answer for a prompt | Constrained-output tasks give false positives (noted by Fu et al.) — [arXiv 2402.15938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15938); [arXiv 2410.18966](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18966) |
| Longitudinal regression at the cutoff (Roberts et al.; Huang et al.; Li & Flanigan) | Black-box + dated items | Items exist on both sides of a (correctly known) cutoff and are otherwise comparable | Performance depends on pre-cutoff exposure proxies → contamination and/or memorization | Latent confounders (difficulty drift); cutoff uncertainty; needs longitudinal data — [arXiv 2310.10628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10628) |
| ConStat (Dekoninck et al.) | Black-box scores | A valid reference benchmark and uncontaminated-enough reference models exist | Statistically significant *non-generalizing* performance (with effect size) | Contaminated reference models; quality of synthetic reference data — [arXiv 2405.16281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16281) |

**Per-paper details**

- **Golchin & Surdeanu (2023/2024), "Time Travel in LLMs: Tracing Data Contamination in Large Language Models"**, ICLR 2024 Spotlight, arXiv:2308.08493 **[V-arXiv]**. *Guided instruction* = dataset name + partition + random-length initial segment of an instance (plus its label when available); *general instruction* omits dataset/partition name. Partition-level decision: Algorithm 1 — ROUGE-L/BLEURT overlap significantly higher under guided vs general instruction (bootstrap); Algorithm 2 — GPT-4 few-shot classifier flags ≥1 exact or ≥2 near-exact matches in a sample of 10 instances. Evaluated in 28 scenarios (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 × 7 datasets × train and test/validation splits); best method (guided instruction + GPT-4 ICL) agrees with human experts 92%–100%; finds GPT-4 contaminated with AG News, WNLI, XSum. Limitation (authors): the best method "does not distinguish between different types of contamination"; relies on small samples and on a GPT-4 judge. — [arXiv 2308.08493](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08493)
- **Golchin & Surdeanu (2023→2025), "Data Contamination Quiz: A Tool to Detect and Estimate Contamination in Large Language Models"**, accepted at TACL (arXiv comment on v7, 28 Apr 2025), arXiv:2311.06233 **[V-arXiv]**. Five-option quiz: original instance vs. GPT-4-generated word-level perturbations plus "None of the provided options"; a Bias Detector Quiz measures positional bias first; output is a min–max range of detected contamination. Works without training data or weights/logits; authors report it uncovers more memorization than prior methods and can bypass some safety filters for copyrighted content. Scope explicitly "verbatim data contamination". — [arXiv 2311.06233](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06233)
- **Deng, Zhao, Tang, Gerstein, Cohan (2023/2024), "Investigating Data Contamination in Modern Benchmarks for Large Language Models"**, NAACL 2024, arXiv:2311.09783 **[V-arXiv]**. (a) Retrieval system for overlap between benchmarks and pre-training corpora; (b) **TS-Guessing** (Testset Slot Guessing): mask a wrong multiple-choice option (Question-Multichoice) or an unlikely word (Question-based) and ask the model to fill it. Abstract: ChatGPT and GPT-4 reach exact-match 52% and 57% on guessing the missing MMLU option; after deliberately contaminating ChatGPT with MMLU the EM "nearly reaches 100"; TruthfulQA performance rises markedly when benchmark metadata is supplied. *Flag:* the body text also says "ChatGPT … achieving a 57% Exact Match" and that GPT-4 showed "only a 1% improvement" over ChatGPT — the abstract and body are not fully consistent on which model has which number. — [arXiv 2311.09783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09783)
- **Oren, Meister, Chatterji, Ladhak, Hashimoto (2023/2024), "Proving Test Set Contamination in Black Box Language Models"**, ICLR 2024 (oral; Outstanding Paper Honorable Mention per ICLR virtual site via search; proceedings title uses "Black-Box"), arXiv:2310.17623 **[V]**. — [arXiv 2310.17623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17623); [ICLR 2024 proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/46e624c244cff669223d488defd4e835-Abstract-Conference.html)
  - Idea: under the null of no contamination, all orderings of an *exchangeable* dataset are equally likely; a model that memorized the dataset prefers the canonical ordering. Permutation test, plus a more powerful **sharded rank/likelihood comparison test** (one-sided t-test over shards) with asymptotic false-positive control. Needs only log-probability queries (no weights, no training data). — [arXiv 2310.17623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17623)
  - Validation: 1.4B-parameter GPT-2 trained on Wikitext + canary test sets; near-zero p-values at duplication ≥10 (e.g. 1.96e-11 on MNLI), works on test sets of ~1,000 examples; "cannot reliably detect duplication rates of 1", detection threshold "around a duplication rate of 4". — [arXiv 2310.17623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17623)
  - Audit of LLaMA2-7B, Mistral-7B, Pythia-1.4B, GPT-2 XL (BioMedLM as negative control) on 8 benchmarks: no evidence of pervasive verbatim contamination; one exception, AI2-ARC on Mistral (p = 0.001), which the authors say is at the boundary after multiple-testing correction; MMLU p-values small ("mild" contamination, heuristic because ≥14 MMLU files are non-exchangeable). Authors: "failure to reject the null hypothesis is not direct evidence in favor of the null"; results are "promising first steps towards third-party audits … rather than a direct proof of contamination of specific models". — [arXiv 2310.17623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17623)
- **Roberts, Thakur, Herlihy, White, Dooley (2023/2024), "To the Cutoff... and Beyond? A Longitudinal Perspective on LLM Data Contamination"**, ICLR 2024, arXiv:2310.10628 **[V]**. *Citation flag:* the arXiv record is titled **"Data Contamination Through the Lens of Time"**; the ICLR 2024 version carries the "To the Cutoff…" title. — [arXiv 2310.10628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10628); [ICLR 2024 poster page](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2024/poster/17911)
  - Method: use the training cutoff (Sept 2021 for GPT-4 / GPT-3.5-Turbo) as a natural experiment on longitudinal benchmarks — Codeforces (2010–2023) and Project Euler (2001–2023) — regressing pass rate on log GitHub presence and difficulty, separately pre/post cutoff. — [arXiv 2310.10628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10628)
  - Result: GitHub presence is significant only *before* the cutoff: for GPT-4 each unit of log GitHub presence raises the pass-rate odds ratio by 4.5% (Codeforces) and 47.8% (Project Euler); for GPT-3.5-Turbo 2.5% and 27.7%; no significant association after the cutoff. Project Euler has only 73 post-cutoff problems. — [arXiv 2310.10628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10628)
  - Limitations (authors): "we cannot rule out the presence of latent confounder(s)"; most benchmarks are released all at once so cannot be analysed this way; private or post-cutoff benchmarks are "time-bound in their utility". Recommends staggered, continuous-integration-style benchmark release. — [arXiv 2310.10628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10628)
- **Shi, Ajith, Xia, Huang, Liu, Blevins, Chen, Zettlemoyer (2023/2024), "Detecting Pretraining Data from Large Language Models"**, ICLR 2024 (PDF header), arXiv:2310.16789 **[V]**. **Min-K% Prob**: average log-likelihood of the k% lowest-probability tokens; hypothesis that "an unseen example is likely to contain a few outlier words with low probabilities". **WikiMIA** benchmark uses events before/after training for ground truth. AUC 0.72 on WikiMIA on average (7.4% above best baseline); AUC 0.88 for copyrighted-book detection on GPT-3; in a controlled downstream-contamination study (LLaMA-7B fine-tuned on RedPajama text with 0.1% benchmark tokens) Min-K% beats baselines. — [arXiv 2310.16789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16789)
  - Known weakness (others): Duan et al. (COLM 2024) find MIAs "barely outperform random guessing" on Pythia/Pile pre-training data (AUC < 0.6 in most domains) and attribute apparent successes to temporal distribution shift between members and non-members — exactly the WikiMIA construction. — [arXiv 2402.07841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07841)
- **Li (2023), "Estimating Contamination via Perplexity: Quantifying Memorisation in Language Model Evaluation"**, arXiv:2309.10677 **[V-arXiv; no venue found]**. Perplexity-based contamination estimate without the training set; reports significant memorisation on reading-comprehension and summarisation benchmarks, less on multiple-choice ones. — [arXiv 2309.10677](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10677)
- **Xu, Wang, Fan, Liu (2024), "Benchmarking Benchmark Leakage in Large Language Models"** (BenBench), arXiv:2404.18824 **[V-arXiv; no venue found]**. Pipeline using **perplexity and N-gram accuracy** on original vs paraphrased benchmark data; 31 LLMs on math reasoning (GSM8K, MATH); e.g. Qwen-1.8B "can accurately predict all 5-grams in 223 examples from the GSM8K training set and 67 from the MATH training set", plus 25 in the MATH test set. Proposes a "Benchmark Transparency Card". Limitation (authors): perplexity gap alone cannot determine extent of leakage; many flags concern the *training* split, which is task contamination rather than test leakage. — [arXiv 2404.18824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18824)
- **Dong, Jiang, Liu, Jin, Gu, Yang, Li (2024), "Generalization or Memorization: Data Contamination and Trustworthy Evaluation for Large Language Models"**, "Accepted to ACL" (arXiv comment) **[V-arXiv; UNVERIFIED-track]**, arXiv:2402.15938. **CDD** (Contamination Detection via output Distribution): sample outputs, measure peakedness via edit distance; **TED** corrects the output distribution. CDD: 21.8%–30.2% average relative improvement over other detectors; TED removes up to 66.9% of contamination-attributed gains; reports potential contamination of ChatGPT on HumanEval. — [arXiv 2402.15938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15938)
- **Dekoninck, Müller, Vechev (2024), "ConStat: Performance-Based Contamination Detection in Large Language Models"**, NeurIPS 2024 (Advances in NeurIPS 37, per ETH record/NeurIPS poster page via search), arXiv:2405.16281 **[V]**. Compare a model's score on the benchmark with its score on a reference benchmark (rephrased / LLM-generated synthetic / different benchmark for the same task), relative to a set of reference models; hardness-correction via smoothing spline; bootstrap p-value and effect size δ. — [arXiv 2405.16281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16281); [NeurIPS 2024 poster](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/poster/96266)
  - Controlled study: detects 89.1% of syntax-contaminated and 98.4% of sample-contaminated fine-tuned models (best baseline 84.8% / 70.5%; Shi et al. baseline 21.7% / 16.4%). Field findings: sample-specific contamination for Llama-3-70b on ARC (δ ≈ 6.6%), Mistral-7b-v0.1 on GSM8k (δ ≈ 8.2%) and Hellaswag (≈3.1%), Llama-2-Instruct-70b on Hellaswag (≈3.4%), Yi-34b on ARC/Hellaswag; top-3 7B Open LLM Leaderboard models show significant benchmark-specific contamination. — [arXiv 2405.16281](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16281)
  - Limitations (authors): results are relative to possibly contaminated reference models; synthetic samples may have label errors or favour the generator model. — [arXiv 2405.16281](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16281)
- **Dekoninck, Müller, Baader, Fischer, Vechev (2024), "Evading Data Contamination Detection for Language Models is (too) Easy"**, arXiv:2402.02823 **[V-arXiv; UNVERIFIED-venue — no peer-reviewed venue found; the PDF uses an ICML-style template]**. Classifies providers (proactive, honest-but-negligent, malicious incl. *evasively malicious*) and detectors (by access: black/grey/white-box/oracle; metadata reliance; sample- vs benchmark-level). **EAL (Evasive Augmentation Learning)**: fine-tune on GPT-4-rephrased benchmark samples mixed with background data. Gains "up to 15%" on contaminated benchmarks while no sample-level detector exceeds 1.6% TPR@1%FPR; n-gram oracle check flags <1% of the contaminated data. — [arXiv 2402.02823](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02823)
- **Singh, Kocyigit, Poulton, Esiobu, Lomeli, Szilvasy, Hupkes (2024), "Evaluation data contamination in LLMs: how do we measure it and (when) does it matter?"**, arXiv:2411.03923 **[V-arXiv; no venue found]**. **ConTAM**: judge n-gram contamination metrics by *Estimated Performance Gain* (do flagged samples actually give the model an advantage?). 13 benchmarks, 7 models (Llama 1, Pythia) with open corpora. For the largest Llama-1 model, estimated gains exceed 15% on HumanEval (18%) and Big Bench Hard (25%), with ≥10 points on HellaSwag, MMLU, PiQA; Pythia gains ~2–8. Longest-match beats union-of-matches; large n and ignoring rare matches produce false negatives; thresholds must be model- and benchmark-specific. Limitation: requires the pre-training corpus. — [arXiv 2411.03923](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03923)
- **Riddell, Ni, Cohan (2024), "Quantifying Contamination in Evaluating Code Generation Capabilities of Language Models"**, arXiv:2403.04811 **[V-arXiv; venue not confirmed]**. Surface- and semantic-level matching of MBPP/HumanEval against open pre-training corpora; substantial overlap, and models do significantly better on problems with similar solutions seen in training. — [arXiv 2403.04811](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04811)

### Inferences
- For closed API models without log-probs (the likely setting for the fraud agent), only black-box tests are available: guided completion / DCQ-style quizzes on filing text, TS-Guessing-style masked-entity recovery, chronological regressions, and ConStat-style reference comparisons. Oren's exchangeability test is inapplicable twice over: no canonical-ordered dataset and no log-probs.
- The detection target for the project differs from the benchmark setting: one wants to detect *knowledge of the outcome associated with an entity/period*, not memorization of a test file. The nearest analogues are entity/date recovery from masked text and date-only recall probes (Lopez-Lira et al.; Gao et al.; §7), combined with a Roberts-style "exposure proxy × pre/post cutoff" interaction (e.g., news coverage volume of the scandal as the analogue of GitHub presence).
- A defensible claim structure, following Li & Flanigan: combine one high-recall/low-precision test (chronological split) with high-precision/low-recall tests (recall probes), and never interpret a null on the latter as cleanliness.

### Gaps
- No detector in this literature has validated power for "label known as world knowledge, input paraphrasable" contamination; validation is nearly always on verbatim leakage with duplication ≥ 4–10.
- Venue for "Evading Data Contamination Detection…" could not be confirmed (author publication page blocked).
- DCQ per-dataset contamination estimates were not extracted (only method and scope verified).

## 3. Evidence that contamination changes measured performance

### Takeaway
The cleanest evidence comes from (a) freshly written matched test sets (GSM1k: drops up to 8 points, concentrated in particular model families; Retro-Misconceptions: up to 16 points), (b) before/after-cutoff comparisons on dated problems (GPT-4 on easy Codeforces: 81% → 12%), and (c) controlled contamination experiments. Effects are heterogeneous: frontier models often show little gap on simple re-written benchmarks, and part of the measured drop in template-perturbation studies is a robustness effect rather than contamination.

### Cited Findings
- **Zhang, Da, Lee, Robinson, Wu, Song, Zhao, Raja, Zhuang, Slack, Lyu, Hendryx, Kaplan, Lunati, Yue (2024), "A Careful Examination of Large Language Model Performance on Grade School Arithmetic"** (GSM1k), NeurIPS 2024 Datasets & Benchmarks Track, arXiv:2405.00332 **[V-arXiv]**. Human-written (no LLM assistance) GSM8k look-alike matched on human solve rate, steps, answer magnitude. *Version flag:* v1 (May 2024) reports **1,250** problems and drops "up to 13%"; the camera-ready v4 (Nov 2024) reports **1,205** problems and drops "up to 8%" — cite the version used. — [arXiv 2405.00332](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00332); [v4 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00332v4)
  - v4 numbers: largest drops Yi-6B-Chat 8.0 points (43.7% → 35.7%), math-shepherd-mistral-7b-rl 7.2, phi-2 6.3, Llama-3-8B-Instruct 6.2; Phi and Mistral families overfit at almost every size; frontier models show minimal gap; Spearman correlation 0.36 (p = 0.03) between a model's per-character log-likelihood of GSM8k test items and its GSM8k–GSM1k gap. — [arXiv 2405.00332v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00332v4)
  - Limitations: sets only approximately matched; prompt-format sensitivity; closed models received GSM1k via API; modest correlation; authors note contamination "is likely not the full explanation". GSM1k was held private with a conditional release plan. — [arXiv 2405.00332v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00332v4)
- **Mirzadeh, Alizadeh, Shahrokhi, Tuzel, Bengio, Farajtabar (2024/2025), "GSM-Symbolic: Understanding the Limitations of Mathematical Reasoning in Large Language Models"**, ICLR 2025, arXiv:2410.05229 **[V]**. 100 symbolic templates × 50 instantiations (5,000 examples; 50 sets of 100). Performance of all models drops on GSM-Symbolic vs GSM8K ("hinting at potential data contamination"), with noticeable variance across instantiations (e.g., >12 points between best and worst set for Gemma2-9B); changing numbers hurts more than changing names; adding one irrelevant clause (GSM-NoOp) causes drops of up to 65%. Limitation for contamination claims: authors themselves interpret much of the effect as fragile reasoning, and drops for e.g. Llama3-8b and GPT-4o are small. — [arXiv 2410.05229](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05229)
- **Huang, Lin, Liu, Gong, Lu, Lei, Liang, Shen, Lin, Duan, Chen (2023/2024), "Competition-Level Problems are Effective LLM Evaluators"**, "ACL 2024" per arXiv comment **[V-arXiv; UNVERIFIED-track]**, arXiv:2312.02143. ~4,957 Codeforces problems (Feb 2010–Nov 2023). GPT-4 (gpt-4-0613) acceptance: easy problems 81.42% (Oct 2010–Sep 2021) → 11.73% (Oct 2021–Nov 2023); medium 43.72% → 0.00%; hard 11.41% → 0.00% — a "cliff like decline" at the September 2021 cutoff across difficulties and problem types. CodeLlama-34B and DeepSeek-Coder-33B show analogous drops around their own cutoffs. Fine-tuning, CoT (11.54% → 16.21% on easy), and problem simplification do not close the gap. - **Roberts et al. (ICLR 2024)**: pass rate tied to GitHub presence only pre-cutoff (numbers in §2). — [arXiv 2310.10628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10628)
- **Li & Flanigan (AAAI 2024)**: pre- vs post-collection datasets (numbers in §1). — [arXiv 2312.16337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.16337)
- **Jain et al. (LiveCodeBench, ICLR 2025)**: DeepSeek-Coder-33B base Pass@1 on LeetCode falls from ~60 (May 2023 problems) to ~0 (Sep 2023 problems), i.e., right after its data cutoff; GPT-4-O drops on problems released after its November 2023 cutoff; fine-tuned open models that excel on HumanEval+ fall >20 points behind on LCB-Easy (e.g., DS-Ins-1.3B 59.8% vs 26.3%). — [arXiv 2403.07974](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07974)

Builds a retrospective holdout for TruthfulQA (Retro-Misconceptions), statistically indistinguishable from the original; across 20 LLMs some scores are inflated by as much as 16 percentage points. — [arXiv 2410.09247](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09247)
- **Wu, Qiu, Ross, Akyürek, Chen, Wang, Kim, Andreas, Kim (2023/2024), "Reasoning or Reciting? - **Singh et al. (2024)**: with open corpora, estimated contamination-attributable gains of 18% (HumanEval) and 25% (Big Bench Hard) for the largest Llama-1 (see §2). — [arXiv 2411.03923](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03923)
- **Balunović, Dekoninck, Petrov, Jovanović, Vechev (2025), "MathArena: Evaluating LLMs on Uncontaminated Math Competitions"**, NeurIPS 2025 (PDF footer) **[V-arXiv; UNVERIFIED-track]**, arXiv:2505.23281. Real-time evaluation on newly released competitions; "strong signs of contamination in AIME 2024"; >50 models, 7 competitions, 162 problems. — [arXiv 2505.23281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23281)
- **Liang, Garg, Zilouchian Moghaddam (2025), "The SWE-Bench Illusion: When State-of-the-Art LLMs Remember Instead of Reason"**, arXiv:2506.12286 **[V-arXiv; no venue found]**. Models identify the buggy file path from the issue text alone with up to 76% accuracy on SWE-Bench Verified vs up to 53% on repositories outside SWE-Bench; up to 35% consecutive 5-gram overlap in function reproduction vs up to 18% on other benchmarks. — [arXiv 2506.12286](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12286)
- **Dominguez-Olmedo et al. (ICLR 2025)**: +7 (MMLU) / +19 (GSM8K) points for post-Nov-2023 models at equal compute, eliminated by equalizing task-specific fine-tuning — i.e., a large part of "newer is better" is task familiarity, not test-item memorization. — [arXiv 2407.07890](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07890)
- **Magar & Schwartz (ACL 2022)**: contamination effect requires many duplicates and is not guaranteed by memorization (numbers in §1). — [arXiv 2203.08242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08242)

### Inferences
- The before/after-cutoff "cliff" is the single most persuasive design in this literature and is directly portable: compare fraud-detection performance on filings whose fraud was *publicly revealed before* vs *after* the model's cutoff, controlling for difficulty (Li & Flanigan's and Roberts's caveat) and for the uncertainty of the cutoff itself (§5).
- Size of effect scales with exposure: Oren, Magar & Schwartz and Roberts all imply that heavily duplicated items are the ones exploited. Enron/WorldCom-class frauds are the fraud-domain analogue of maximally duplicated benchmark items; obscure AAER cases are closer to duplication-count-1 items. A stratified analysis by notoriety is therefore more informative than a pooled one.
- GSM-Symbolic and Wu et al. warn that a performance drop under perturbation conflates contamination with brittleness; a perturbation control on *unknown* firms is needed to net out the brittleness component.

### Gaps
- No verified study found that measures contamination effects on a *document-level classification with real-world outcome labels* task comparable to fraud detection from 10-Ks; all quantitative evidence is from math, code, QA, or classification benchmarks (finance-forecasting evidence is in §7).
- Track for Huang et al. (ACL main vs Findings) and MathArena (NeurIPS main vs D&B) not confirmed.

## 4. Mitigation strategies and their strengths / weaknesses

### Takeaway
Mitigations are (1) *prevention* (encryption, licences, canary strings, private test sets), (2) *temporal* (post-cutoff / continuously refreshed benchmarks), (3) *regeneration* (rule-based or LLM-based perturbation, paraphrase, counterfactual variants, interactive evaluation), and (4) *adjustment* (ConStat-style correction, inference-time decontamination). The first controlled comparison of 20 regeneration strategies (Sun et al., ICML 2025) found none that both preserves what the benchmark measures and resists contamination; temporal splits are the most trusted but are expensive, time-bound, and depend on knowing the true cutoff.

### Cited Findings

**Prevention**
- Jacovi et al. (EMNLP 2023): encryption + no-derivatives licence, training-exclusion controls, avoid data with online solutions; defeated by negligent re-uploading. — [arXiv 2305.10160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10160)
- **Canary strings** (BIG-bench GUID; Srivastava et al., arXiv:2206.04615 — not re-verified in full, cited via search summary): intended to let developers filter benchmark files from training corpora and to diagnose training-on-test post hoc. Chen et al.'s survey notes canaries only work if trainers check for and respect them. An informal (non-peer-reviewed) LessWrong investigation reports that GPT-4-base reproduces the BIG-bench canary string. — [arXiv 2206.04615](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04615); [arXiv 2502.17521v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17521v2); [LessWrong post](https://lesswrong.com/posts/kSmHMoaLKGcGgyWzs/big-bench-canary-contamination-in-gpt-4)
- **Private / held-out test sets**: GSM1k kept private pending a release condition; LiveBench keeps the newest 1/6 of questions private for a month; Dekoninck et al. note private benchmarks avoid leakage but "cannot be run by the model provider" and are problematic for closed models; Roberts et al. warn private benchmarks "may further erode transparency". — [arXiv 2405.00332v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00332v4); [arXiv 2406.19314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19314); [arXiv 2402.02823](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02823); [arXiv 2310.10628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10628)

**Do the mitigations work? (critical evaluations)**
- **Sun, Wang, Li, Wang, Zhang (2025), "The Emperor's New Clothes in Benchmarking? A Rigorous Examination of Mitigation Strategies for LLM Benchmark Data Contamination"**, ICML 2025 (PMLR 267:57728–57753), arXiv:2503.16402 **[V]**. Two question-level metrics: **fidelity** (updated benchmark gives the same per-question results as the original for a clean model) and **contamination resistance** (results on the updated benchmark are unchanged by contaminating the model on the original). 10 LLMs × 5 benchmarks × 20 strategies × 2 contamination recipes: no strategy significantly improves resistance over the vanilla (no update) case across all benchmarks, and none balances fidelity and resistance. — [arXiv 2503.16402](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16402); [PMLR v267](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/sun25t.html)
- Chen et al. survey: no existing dynamic benchmark satisfies all six proposed criteria; temporal-cutoff benchmarks need continual human effort; rule-based ones risk in-distribution contamination once published; LLM-generated ones have uncertain sample quality. — [arXiv 2502.17521v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17521v2)
- Roberts et al.: private and post-cutoff benchmarks are "likely to be time-bound in their utility and may be cost-prohibitive to sustain". — [arXiv 2310.10628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10628)
- Dekoninck et al.: dynamic benchmarks are costly and vary over time; human evaluation is expensive and manipulable; post-hoc "clean subset" evaluation is insufficient. — [arXiv 2402.02823](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02823)

### Inferences
- For the fraud project, the "regenerate the benchmark" family maps to *anonymizing / perturbing filings*; Sun et al.'s fidelity-vs-resistance trade-off predicts the same dilemma: stripping identity and numbers enough to block recall may also change the task (confirmed empirically in finance by Wu, Yang, Ying, Zhou 2025 — §7).
- The "temporal" family maps to evaluating on frauds revealed after the model's cutoff. Its special weakness here is label latency: fraud is typically revealed years after the filing, so genuinely post-cutoff *labelled* cases are few, giving the low-power problem LiveCodeBench reports (349 problems → 1–1.5% noise) in a much more severe form.
- A reasonable design is therefore layered: (i) a small post-cutoff primary test, (ii) a larger pre-cutoff set analysed with exposure-stratified and ConStat-style reference comparisons, (iii) counterfactual/perturbed filings as a diagnostic rather than as the headline number.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of canary-string effectiveness was found; evidence is design documentation plus an informal blog post.
- BIG-bench paper details (canary design) were not re-verified from the paper itself.
- Venue of ITD (arXiv:2406.13990) and Zhou et al. not confirmed.

## 5. Knowledge-cutoff ambiguity

### Takeaway
A model's *reported* cutoff is not the date its knowledge of a given resource actually reflects. "Effective cutoffs" are resource-specific and frequently years earlier than reported (because crawls contain stale copies and deduplication fails), while small amounts of post-cutoff data can also be present. Time-split evaluations therefore need a margin on both sides of the nominal cutoff.

### Cited Findings
- **Cheng, Marone, Weller, Lawrie, Khashabi, Van Durme (2024), "Dated Data: Tracing Knowledge Cutoffs in Large Language Models"**, COLM 2024 (PDF header "Published as a conference paper at COLM 2024"), arXiv:2403.12958 **[V]**. — [arXiv 2403.12958](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12958)
  - Definitions: *reported cutoff* = date advertised by the model creator; *effective cutoff* = "the date of the version of the resource that is most closely aligned with the model", defined per resource. — [arXiv 2403.12958v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12958v2)
  - Method: perplexity over time-versioned documents (WikiSpan: ~5,000 Wikipedia pages monthly Apr 2016–Apr 2023; NewsSpan: NYT articles Jan 2016–Jul 2020), min-max scaled; the effective cutoff is the month of minimum relative perplexity. — [arXiv 2403.12958v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12958v2)
  - Findings: Pile-based models (Pythia, GPT-Neo, GPT-J) align with the Pile's March 2020 Wikipedia dump; FalconRW's minimum is around Jan 2020 vs. a reported date of Feb 2023; RedPajamas ≈ 2019 despite an explicit March 2023 Wikipedia dump; "over 80%" of Wikipedia documents in RedPajamas are pre-2023 versions. Causes: (1) temporal misalignment of CommonCrawl dumps (lots of old data in new crawls); (2) deduplication failures on semantic/near duplicates. — [arXiv 2403.12958v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12958v2)
  - Limitations: needs token log-probabilities; minima often not sharp (better seen as a distribution); closed-model results speculative; NYT data ends mid-2020. — [arXiv 2403.12958v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12958v2)
- Leakage in the other direction: Roberts et al. note "GPT-4 acknowledges training with some small amount of data beyond its cutoff … so post-cutoff examples may still appear", and find GitHub presence still weakly significant post-cutoff for GPT-3.5-Turbo, suggesting more exposure after the nominal cutoff. — [arXiv 2310.10628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10628)
- AntiLeak-Bench's premise: post-cutoff *documents* may contain pre-cutoff *knowledge*, so recency of collection does not guarantee novelty. — [arXiv 2412.13670](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13670)
- Finance evidence consistent with sharp-but-not-perfect cutoffs: Gao, Jiang, Yan find their recall-propensity statistic is materially positive in-sample and "collapses essentially to zero" in the year after Llama-3.3-70B's December 2023 cutoff; Lopez-Lira, Tang, Zhu find GPT-4o headline-dating accuracy for the year falls from 98.45% pre-cutoff to 28.81% post-cutoff. — [arXiv 2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23847); [arXiv 2504.14765](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v2)
- Indirect leakage after deployment (Sainz et al.; Balloccu et al.) means a model snapshot's effective knowledge can also be shaped by user-submitted evaluation data. — [arXiv 2310.18018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18018); [arXiv 2402.03927](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03927)

### Inferences
- For an Enron-FY2000-type case the cutoff ambiguity is irrelevant (the outcome is decades before any cutoff and massively duplicated). It matters for the *clean* end of the sample: cases revealed within roughly a year either side of the reported cutoff should be treated as "uncertain exposure" and excluded or analysed separately.
- "Effective cutoff earlier than reported" implies that under-representation of the most recent pre-cutoff events makes very recent frauds *less* contaminated than the nominal cutoff suggests — exposure should be modelled as a gradient (as in Roberts's GitHub-presence regressor), not a step.

### Gaps
- No verified method estimates effective cutoffs for closed frontier models without log-probs; Cheng et al.'s closed-model results are explicitly speculative.
- Not found: a study estimating effective cutoffs specifically for SEC/EDGAR or financial-news resources.

## 6. State of the art 2025–2026: does detection work? emerging consensus and critiques

### Takeaway
By 2025–2026 the weight of evidence is that (a) instance-level, memorization-based detectors are unreliable on pre-training data and are trivially evaded by paraphrase, RL post-training, or CoT-style fine-tuning; (b) contamination has broadened from verbatim leakage to "soft"/semantic duplicates that n-gram decontamination misses; (c) regeneration-based mitigations do not deliver; hence (d) the field is converging on performance-based, time-split, and continuously refreshed evaluation, with position papers calling for contamination-resistant benchmark design. No detection method is accepted as a general-purpose certificate of cleanliness.

### Cited Findings
- **Fu, Uzuner, Yetisgen, Xia (Findings of NAACL 2025)**, arXiv:2410.18966: reviewed 50 detection papers; eight assumption categories (A1 absolute probability; A2 perturbation-relative probability incl. exchangeability; A3 reference-model-relative probability; A4 verbatim continuation; A5 key-information regeneration; A6 low output variation; A7 metadata-conditioned generation; A8 performance drop under answer/option perturbation). Prior work already shows A1, A2, A3, A7 can fail; A5 and A8 unverified. Case study (Pythia, OLMo-2-7B, Zephyr-7B-β, BioMistral-NLU-7B): for pre-training data all tested metrics give AUC ≈ 50 (averages 49.0–51.8); on fine-tuning data results range up to 99.4 AUC; cross-domain experiments show metrics track *domain fit*, not instance membership. Authors' reading: LLMs may "learn general data distributions rather than memorize individual instances". Limitations: only three assumptions tested; English text; none of the top-10 leaderboard models disclosed training data. — [arXiv 2410.18966](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18966v3)
- **Samuel, Zhou, Zou (COLING 2025), "Towards Data Contamination Detection for Modern Large Language Models: Limitations, Inconsistencies, and Oracle Challenges"**, arXiv:2409.09927 **[V-arXiv]**: five detectors × four SOTA LLMs × eight datasets, plus an oracle (LLaMA-2-70B deliberately contaminated via instruction fine-tuning with answer augmentation). Findings: all methods have limiting assumptions; contamination from instruction fine-tuning with answer augmentation is especially hard to detect; SOTA detectors show limited agreement with one another ("cannot be simultaneously valid"). — [arXiv 2409.09927](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09927)
- **Duan, Suri, Mireshghallah, Min, Shi, Zettlemoyer, Tsvetkov, Choi, Evans, Hajishirzi (COLM 2024), "Do Membership Inference Attacks Work on Large Language Models?"**, arXiv:2402.07841 **[V]**: MIAs barely beat random on pre-training data; apparent successes explained by temporal distribution shift. (Only the evaluation-relevant conclusion is used here; MIA theory is out of scope.) — [arXiv 2402.07841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07841)
- **Dekoninck et al. (2024)** EAL: rephrased-sample fine-tuning inflates scores up to 15% while evading all tested detectors. — [arXiv 2402.02823](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02823)
- **Wang, Li, Ko, Zhang (2025/2026), "On The Fragility of Benchmark Contamination Detection in Reasoning Models"**, ICLR 2026, arXiv:2510.02386 **[V]**. (I) SFT contamination is initially detectable (reference-free detectors ≈ 73.42% AUROC; a reference-based detector 89.13% on average over six benchmarks, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) but brief GRPO reinforcement-learning training conceals it, with AUROC falling as GRPO steps increase (≤156 steps tested); SFT contamination inflated pass@1 by a further 8.82% on average over clean SFT. (II) SFT contamination with CoT applied to advanced reasoning models leaves most detectors near random. Authors attribute concealment to PPO-style importance sampling/clipping. — [arXiv 2510.02386](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02386)
- **Spiesberger, Vazquez, Pochinkov, Gavenčiak, Grietzer, Leech, Schoots (2026), "Soft Contamination Means Benchmarks Test Shallow Generalization"**, arXiv:2602.12413 (v2 7 Nov 2026) **[V-arXiv; UNVERIFIED-venue — PDF carries a NeurIPS 2026 footer but the arXiv record lists no acceptance]**. Embeds the full Olmo 3 post-training corpus: semantic duplicates exist for 76% of the CodeForces test set and 100% of MBPP; fine-tuning on semantic duplicates raises held-out accuracy by up to 22 points (10–11 at realistic rates), mostly without transfer to related benchmarks ("shallow generalization"); at realistic rates soft contamination can match or exceed exact duplicates. — [arXiv 2602.12413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12413)
- **Wu et al. (14 authors; AAAI 2026), "Reasoning or Memorization? Unreliable Results of Reinforcement Learning Due to Data Contamination"**, arXiv:2507.10532 **[V-arXiv]**: reported RL gains (even with random/incorrect rewards) on Qwen2.5 for MATH-500/AMC/AIME are attributed to contamination; on a leakage-free generator (RandomCalculation) only correct rewards help. — [arXiv 2507.10532](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.10532)
- **Sun et al. (ICML 2025)**: none of 20 mitigation strategies beats "no update" on contamination resistance across benchmarks (see §4). — [PMLR v267](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/sun25t.html)
- **Ravaut et al. (TMLR 2025)** survey's own caveats: MIA near-random on pre-training data; Min-K% sensitive to K and token frequency; RLHF smooths confidence and weakens signals; EAL evades even oracle-access methods. — [arXiv 2404.00699v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.00699v5)
- **Al-Lawati, Lucas, Lee, Wang (2026), "LLM Benchmark Datasets Should Be Contamination-Resistant"**, ICML 2026 Position Paper Track (arXiv comment), arXiv:2605.19999 **[V-arXiv]**: position that benchmarks should be hard to learn from in training yet usable at inference, exploiting the training/inference asymmetry of Transformers; calls for methods, tooling, and adoption. No quantitative results in the abstract. — [arXiv 2605.19999](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19999)
- **Lan, Cao, Wang, Lin, Chen (2026), "The Illusion of Reasoning: Exposing Evasive Data Contamination in LLMs via Zero-CoT Truncation"**, arXiv:2605.21856 **[V-arXiv; preprint]**: black-box Zero-CoT Probe comparing no-CoT performance on original vs. isomorphically perturbed items, with a "Contamination Confidence" score; targets paraphrase-based (evasive) contamination. — [arXiv 2605.21856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21856)
- **Chai, Zhe, Sakuma (2026), "When Benchmarks Leak: Inference-Time Decontamination for LLMs"** (DeconIEP), arXiv:2601.19334 **[V-arXiv; preprint]**: bounded input-embedding perturbations guided by a less-contaminated reference model. — [arXiv 2601.19334](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19334)
- **Song, An, Chen, Yan, Song, Shao, Li (2026), "Silicon Bureaucracy and AI Test-Oriented Education: Contamination Sensitivity and Score Confidence in LLM Benchmarks"**, arXiv:2603.21636 **[V-arXiv; preprint]**: proposes auditing "contamination sensitivity" and "score confidence" alongside benchmark scores. — [arXiv 2603.21636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21636)
- Institutionalization: the CONDA workshop (ACL 2024) and its shared-task contamination database (566 entries, 91 sources). — [arXiv 2407.21530](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21530)

### Inferences
- The defensible 2026 position for a literature review: detection methods can *establish* contamination when they fire (especially Oren-style tests with error control, or verbatim-recall tests), but cannot *rule it out*; evaluation designs should therefore assume contamination for any pre-cutoff, publicly documented item and rely on design (time splits, reference comparisons, exposure stratification) rather than on detectors.
- The "soft contamination" and "training on the test task" results suggest that even perfectly anonymized well-known fraud cases are not clean if their *fact patterns* are widely discussed (e.g., SPE-based off-balance-sheet financing is a textbook pattern because of Enron). This is semantic-level contamination of the task, not just of the instance.
- Reasoning-model results (Wang et al.) imply likelihood-based probes are least trustworthy precisely for the model class a fraud-analysis agent would likely use.

### Gaps
- No 2026 survey dedicated to contamination was found beyond the 2025 surveys (search-limited; "Beyond the Black Box", arXiv:2601.02907, surfaced via an aggregator but was not opened or verified).
- The 2026 preprints (2605.21856, 2601.19334, 2603.21636) were verified only at abstract level; quantitative claims were not extracted.
- Whether any consensus *reporting standard* (e.g., mandatory contamination statements) has been adopted by major venues was not found.

## 7. Bridge to the project: contamination as look-ahead bias when an LLM reads historical financial text (adjacent scope, kept short)

### Takeaway
The finance/economics literature independently formalizes the project's exact problem: when the model's weights encode what happened after the document date, pre-cutoff "predictions" are not identified as skill vs. recall; prompts asking the model to ignore later knowledge do not work; anonymization is partially effective but both leaky (entities are re-identified from context) and lossy (removes genuine signal). Post-cutoff evaluation and point-in-time ("chronologically consistent") models are the two remedies accepted as clean.

### Cited Findings
- **Lopez-Lira, Tang, Zhu (2025), "The Memorization Problem: Can We Trust LLMs' Economic Forecasts?"**, working paper, arXiv:2504.14765 (v2 15 Dec 2025; also SSRN 5217505) **[V-arXiv; not peer-reviewed as of verification]**. Argues counterfactual forecasting ability is *non-identified* pre-cutoff and that memorization evidence is only a lower bound. GPT-4o: pre-cutoff macro recall MAE 0.03%–0.15% with >96% threshold accuracy vs. post-cutoff GDP MAE 0.95%; headline dating year-accuracy 98.45% pre vs 28.81% post. Fake-cutoff instructions fail (system-prompt-only fake cutoff: 0.05% error, 98% directional accuracy *after* the fake cutoff). Masking: on entity-neutered earnings-call excerpts GPT-4o identifies the company in 100% of Apple/Meta/Microsoft calls and >85% for all Magnificent-7 firms; 93.2% correct quarter+year for Apple; a small-cap example (Ethan Allen) was also identified. Recommends post-cutoff data as the primary analysis and per-setting memorization tests. Limitations: mainly one model; small post-cutoff samples. — [arXiv 2504.14765v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v2)
- **Gao, Jiang, Yan (2025/2026), "Detecting Lookahead Bias in LLM Forecasts"**, working paper, arXiv:2512.23847 (v2 12 Jun 2026) **[V-arXiv]**. Date-only recall query per firm-date gives **Lookahead Propensity (LAP)**; a positive LAP × forecast interaction in an accuracy regression signals contamination; LAP is materially positive in-sample and collapses to ~zero after Llama-3.3-70B's Dec 2023 cutoff; applied to news→returns and earnings calls→capex; interaction loses significance post-cutoff. — [arXiv 2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23847)
- **Glasserman & Lin (2023), "Assessing Look-Ahead Bias in Stock Return Predictions Generated By GPT Sentiment Analysis"**, arXiv:2309.17322 **[V-arXiv]**. Distinguishes *look-ahead bias* from a *distraction effect* (general knowledge of the named company interferes); in-sample, anonymized headlines *outperform* originals, especially for large companies — i.e., entity knowledge can hurt as well as help. — [arXiv 2309.17322](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17322)
- **Sarkar & Vafa (2024), "Lookahead Bias in Pretrained Language Models"**, working paper (SSRN 4754678 per citing papers; NBER conference copy) **[UNVERIFIED-venue; details from search summary only]**: direct tests of temporal leakage in predicting corporate risk factors from earnings calls and election winners from biographies; recommends models pre-trained only on pre-period text. — [NBER conference paper PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f205530/f205530.pdf)
- **He, Lv, Manela, Wu (2025), "Chronologically Consistent Large Language Models"**, arXiv:2502.21206 **[V-arXiv]**: ChronoBERT/ChronoGPT, one model per year from 1999 trained only on text available at the time; news-based return prediction reaches Sharpe ratios comparable to a much larger Llama, "indicating that lookahead bias is modest" in that application; bias is model- and application-specific. — [arXiv 2502.21206](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.21206)
- **Drinkall, Rahimikia, Pierrehumbert, Zohren (2024), "Time Machine GPT"**, Findings of NAACL 2024, arXiv:2404.18543 **[V-arXiv]**: point-in-time ("nonprognosticative") GPT models with released training sets. — [arXiv 2404.18543](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18543)
- **Wu, Yang, Ying, Zhou (2025/2026), "Anonymization and Information Loss"**, arXiv:2511.15364 **[V-arXiv]**: anonymization weakens LLM signals; for S&P credit-downgrade prediction raw text does much better than anonymized text and the gap is *not* explained by look-ahead bias but by loss of numeric detail and named actors; proposes an outcome-free "anonymization gap" metric. — [arXiv 2511.15364](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15364)
- **Engelberg et al. (2025), "Entity Neutering"** **[UNVERIFIED — author list and venue not confirmed; described via citing papers/search]**: LLM agent iteratively masks and paraphrases until an independent LLM cannot identify the firm; reported to reduce identification to about chance on >500,000 news articles. Contradicted in part by Lopez-Lira et al.'s re-identification results on earnings calls. — [search-surfaced description via arXiv 2504.14765v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v2)
- **Wongchamcharoen & Glasserman (2025), "Do Large Language Models (LLMs) Understand Chronology?"**, arXiv:2511.14214 (AAAI-26 AI4TS workshop / student abstract per comment) **[V-arXiv]**: prompt-based look-ahead avoidance presupposes chronological competence; exact-match ordering degrades with list length for earlier models, while GPT-5 at medium/high reasoning effort orders flawlessly. — [arXiv 2511.14214](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14214)
- **Cautionary citation flag — Kim, Muhn, Nikolaev (2024), "Financial Statement Analysis with Large Language Models"**, arXiv:2407.17866: the arXiv record (v3, 20 Feb 2025) is marked **withdrawn**; the comment states a co-author found inconsistencies in data and analyses while replicating, and the working paper was temporarily withdrawn pending review. Its "anonymized statements rule out memorization" argument should not be cited as settled. — [arXiv 2407.17866](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17866)

### Inferences
- Mapping to the general contamination literature: LAP × forecast interaction (Gao et al.) is the finance analogue of Roberts et al.'s GitHub-presence × pre/post-cutoff regression; entity neutering is the analogue of paraphrase-based benchmark regeneration and inherits Sun et al.'s fidelity/resistance trade-off (Wu–Yang–Ying–Zhou show the fidelity loss; Lopez-Lira et al. show the resistance failure).
- For a 10-K, anonymization is harder than for headlines: segment structure, product descriptions, auditor, subsidiaries and magnitudes jointly identify a firm; the re-identification result on earnings calls (>85–100%) suggests re-identification should be *measured* for each neutered filing, with results reported separately for re-identified vs. non-re-identified firms.
- Glasserman & Lin's distraction effect implies contamination may bias the fraud score in either direction (e.g., a famous non-fraud firm being scored as safe from reputation), so the test should be two-sided.

### Gaps
- No verified paper was found that studies contamination/look-ahead bias specifically for LLM *fraud or misstatement detection from 10-K text*; the closest verified settings are returns from news, capex from earnings calls, risk factors, and credit downgrades.
- "Entity Neutering" and Sarkar & Vafa were not verified from primary pages (SSRN not fetched; `icml.cc` blocked).
- Most finance papers here are working papers; peer-review status as of Nov 2026 was not established.
